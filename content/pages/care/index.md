Menopause care usually starts with your primary care doctor, OB/GYN or nurse practitioner; a clinician with extra menopause training helps most when hormone therapy may not be safe for you or the right choice is unclear [@menopause-society-choosing-practitioner; @nice-ng23-recommendations]. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

This section is about the practical side: who to see, how to pay for it, how to be heard, and what helps day to day. Each guide below answers one of those questions in depth.

## Where can I get menopause care?

Most people get menopause care from the clinician they already see: a primary care doctor, OB/GYN, nurse practitioner or physician assistant. If you want someone with tested menopause training, look for a Menopause Society Certified Practitioner (MSCP), a credential open to any licensed clinician who passes the Society's exam [@menopause-society-choosing-practitioner].

- **In person**: The Menopause Society's directory can be searched by US zip code, and it shows which listed clinicians hold the MSCP [@menopause-society-practitioner-directory; @menopause-society-choosing-practitioner]. It only includes people who asked to be listed, and the Society doesn't endorse them [@menopause-society-practitioner-directory].
- **By telehealth**: the same directory can be searched for clinicians offering telehealth in your state [@menopause-society-practitioner-directory].
- **Through a referral**: in many HMO and POS plans you need a referral from your primary care doctor before a specialist visit is covered [@healthcare-gov-plan-types].

## When is a menopause specialist worth it?

A specialist is most worth it when hormones may be risky for you, when it's unclear what will work best, or when your first treatment hasn't helped. Those are the situations in which UK guidance suggests referral to someone with menopause expertise [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Reasons hormone therapy may not be safe include a past blood clot, stroke or heart attack, estrogen-sensitive cancer, liver disease, or unexplained vaginal bleeding [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## What if my doctor dismisses my symptoms?

Ask directly whether menopause has been considered and what rules out treatment, and get a second opinion if the answer isn't clear. Gaps in training are real: in a 2017 survey of US doctors in training, about 1 in 5 had received no menopause teaching, and fewer than 1 in 10 felt prepared to manage menopause [@kling-resident-menopause-knowledge-2019]. NICE says menopause care should be tailored to the person and adapted as symptoms change [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Does insurance cover menopause care, and what does HRT cost?

Most US plans cover menopause visits and prescriptions in some form, but your share depends on the plan. Marketplace plans must cover outpatient care, prescription drugs and lab tests [@healthcare-gov-essential-health-benefits]. All state Medicaid programs currently cover outpatient prescriptions, and Medicare drug plans usually put generics on their cheapest tier [@medicaid-gov-prescription-drugs; @medicare-how-drug-plans-work].

Coverage doesn't always mean access. In a 2026 analysis of US national survey data, women with Medicaid reported ever using menopausal hormone therapy about half as often as women with private insurance (10.7% vs 20.7%) [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]. Without insurance, generic HRT can be fairly cheap: in November 2026, GoodRx average retail prices were $19.94 to $47.05 for 90 estradiol tablets and $37.13 to $68.95 for a carton of 4 weekly patches [@goodrx-estradiol-prices-2026-11-08].

## How do I prepare for a menopause appointment?

Bring a few weeks of symptom notes, your period pattern, your medicines and supplements, and one or two clear questions. Short appointments go further when you can show patterns instead of recalling them on the spot. NICE recommends reviewing any menopause treatment after 3 months and then yearly, so it helps to keep tracking after you start [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

Use the [symptom tracker and doctor summary](/tools/symptom-tracker/) to log symptoms privately on your device and print a one-page summary, or start from our [questions to ask your doctor about perimenopause](/perimenopause/questions-for-your-doctor/).

## What can I do at home?

A few self-care steps have real evidence behind them, and some popular ones are about comfort rather than treatment:

- **Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants**: used regularly, moisturizers are first-line care for vaginal dryness, and lubricants help during sex [@nams-2020-gsm; @acog-clinical-consensus-gsm].
- **Pelvic floor exercises**: :grade{id="pfmt-incontinence"} for stress incontinence. Supervised training helps most women with leaks when coughing or laughing [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence; @cochrane-pfmt-incontinence].
- **Lighter sleepwear and a cooler room**: :grade{id="cooling-comfort"}. They can make nights more comfortable, but cooling doesn't reduce the number of hot flashes [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy], and fabric studies haven't been done in menopausal women [@li-sleepwear-bedding-fibre-review-2024].

## When to get care quickly

:::urgent
- **Any bleeding after menopause**, even a small amount or one episode, needs a doctor's check [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding].
- **Thoughts of harming yourself**: call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) in the US, any time [@lifeline-988].
:::
