Whether your insurance actually covers menopause care — visits, hormone therapy, non-hormonal prescriptions — varies a lot by plan, and the honest answer is that we can't tell you what *your specific plan* covers from here. What we can show you is real data on how much insurance type actually matters for who gets treated.

## What the research shows: insurance type is a real access gap

A 2026 cross-sectional study used NHANES (a nationally representative US health survey) data from 2013–2020 to compare menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) use between women with Medicaid and women with private insurance, among those aged 45–64 eligible for MHT [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]. The results:

- Women with **Medicaid** reported a history of MHT use at **10.7%**, compared with **20.7%** for women with **private insurance** — less than half the rate
- After adjusting for other factors, Medicaid-insured women had significantly **lower odds of MHT use** than privately insured women (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.87)
- The study also found **Black women had lower odds of MHT use than White women**, and this gap was **partly, but not fully, explained** once insurance status was factored in [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]

The researchers frame this plainly: insurance type appears to be a real barrier to menopause care access, and addressing it is part of what equitable menopause care would require [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026].

## What this means practically

If you have Medicaid or are otherwise underinsured, this data suggests you may be starting from a structurally harder position in accessing hormone therapy specifically — not because it isn't appropriate for you, but because of documented, system-level gaps in access. That's worth naming clearly, both so you don't blame yourself for a gap that isn't about you personally, and so you know it's worth persisting (a second opinion, a referral, or a specialist search, covered in our [guide to finding a menopause specialist](/care/find-a-menopause-specialist/)) if your first attempt at care doesn't go well.

## Why some providers don't bill insurance at all

You'll notice some menopause-focused telehealth companies (reviewed in our [telehealth comparisons](/compare/)) use a **cash-pay or membership model** instead of billing insurance. This is usually a deliberate choice to avoid the delays and complexity of insurance pre-authorization, not a sign of being disreputable — but it does mean your actual cost depends on what you'd pay out-of-pocket versus through a covered, in-network provider. Compare both paths before assuming either one is automatically cheaper for you. See our [HRT cost guide](/care/hrt-cost/) for typical price ranges to compare against.

## How to actually check your own coverage

Since plans vary enormously, the most reliable path is:

- Call your insurance plan directly and ask specifically about coverage for menopausal hormone therapy, both brand-name and generic options
- Ask your prescriber's office whether they can check your specific formulary before you fill a prescription
- If a telehealth provider is cash-pay only, ask whether they provide documentation (a superbill) you could submit to your insurer for partial reimbursement
- Prepare these specific questions in our [treatment conversation builder](/tools/treatment-questions/) before you call

:::questions
- Does my specific plan cover generic vs. brand-name hormone therapy differently, and would that change which option makes sense for me?
- If my current access to care isn't working, is a referral to a menopause specialist a realistic next step given my insurance?
:::
