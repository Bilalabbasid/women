US Marketplace health plans must cover outpatient visits and prescription drugs, and every state's Medicaid program covers outpatient prescriptions [@healthcare-gov-essential-health-benefits; @medicaid-gov-prescription-drugs]. What you pay for hormone therapy depends on your plan's drug list. On Medicare drug plans, most generic drugs sit on the cheapest tier [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work].

## What does insurance usually cover for menopause?

Menopause care usually means office visits, sometimes lab tests, and prescriptions. All US Marketplace plans must cover outpatient care, prescription drugs and lab services as "essential health benefits" [@healthcare-gov-essential-health-benefits]. What you pay out of pocket depends on your plan's deductible and copays, and on whether the clinician is in network: HMOs, for example, generally don't cover out-of-network care except in an emergency [@healthcare-gov-plan-types].

- **Visits**: a visit about menopause symptoms is outpatient care, which Marketplace plans must cover [@healthcare-gov-essential-health-benefits]
- **Hormone therapy and other prescriptions**: covered if the drug is on your plan's drug list (formulary) [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications]
- **Medicaid**: all states currently cover outpatient prescription drugs for most enrollees [@medicaid-gov-prescription-drugs]
- **Medicare**: drug coverage comes through a Medicare drug plan, which has its own drug list [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work]

We can't tell you what your own plan covers. The steps below show how to find out.

## Is HRT covered by insurance, generic or brand?

Generic hormones are often covered, and on Medicare drug plans most generics have the lowest copay [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work]. GoodRx says generic progesterone is covered by most Medicare and insurance plans, and estradiol by some [@goodrx-progesterone-pricing-2026; @goodrx-estradiol-prices-2026-11-08]. Coverage still depends on the exact drug, dose and form.

Medicare drug plans show how this pricing usually works [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work]:

- **Tier 1**: the lowest copay, for most generic drugs
- **Tier 2**: preferred brand-name drugs
- **Tier 3**: non-preferred brand-name drugs
- **Specialty tier**: the highest cost, for very high-cost drugs

Plans can change their drug list during the year, so check again when you refill [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work]. Private plans also set their own drug lists, and drugs on the list usually cost you less than drugs that aren't [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications]. For cash prices to compare against, see our [HRT cost guide](/care/hrt-cost/).

## What if my insurance won't cover my HRT?

You can ask your plan for an exception. If a drug isn't on your Marketplace plan's list, you have the right to use the insurer's drug exceptions process, and your prescriber tells the plan why you need that drug [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications]. On Medicare, you or your prescriber can ask the plan to cover a drug that's not on its list, or to charge less for one on a higher tier [@medicare-how-drug-plans-work].

- **Ask your prescriber about covered options**: a covered generic or another form may suit you
- **Request an exception**: your prescriber gives the medical reason, for example that covered drugs would work less well or cause harmful side effects [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications; @medicare-how-drug-plans-work]
- **Appeal a denial**: on a Marketplace plan, an independent third party reviews the appeal [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications]
- **Compare the cash price**: GoodRx listed 90 generic estradiol tablets at about $20 to $47 without insurance in November 2026 [@goodrx-estradiol-prices-2026-11-08]; see our [HRT cost guide](/care/hrt-cost/) for other forms

## How do I check what my plan covers?

The quickest way is to look up your exact prescription on your plan's drug list, or call your insurer. HealthCare.gov suggests the insurer's website, your Summary of Benefits and Coverage, a call to the insurer, or the coverage papers your plan mailed you [@healthcare-gov-prescription-medications]. When you call, ask:

- Is my hormone therapy (drug, dose and form) on your drug list, and on which tier?
- Is the generic cheaper than the brand on my plan?
- Is my clinician in network, and do I need a referral to see a specialist? Many HMOs require one, and the plan may not pay without it [@healthcare-gov-referral-glossary]
- What is the exceptions process if my drug isn't covered?

Use our [treatment conversation builder](/tools/treatment-questions/) to prepare these questions before you call.

## Do telehealth menopause clinics take my insurance?

Some do and some don't, so check each one against your plan. Of the services we cover, Midi bills insurance and says it is in-network with most PPO plans, Evernow accepts insurance for its video visits, and Alloy doesn't bill insurance at all [@midi-health-pricing-2026; @evernow-pricing-2026; @alloy-insurance-2026]. Here's what each one says:

- **[Midi Health](/compare/midi-review/)**: in-network with most PPO plans; Medicare patients are seen as self-pay only, and Medicaid isn't accepted [@midi-health-pricing-2026]
- **[Evernow](/compare/evernow-review/)**: video visits are insurance-eligible, either as a one-time visit or within its membership, and it also takes HSA or FSA funds and self-pay [@evernow-pricing-2026; @evernow-own-site-2026]
- **[Alloy](/compare/alloy-review/)**: doesn't bill insurance, though some patients get reimbursed by PPO plans afterward, and HSA or FSA funds can be used [@alloy-insurance-2026]

Even when a service is in-network, what you pay depends on your own plan's copays, deductible and drug list.

If a service is cash-pay only, ask whether it gives paperwork (often called a superbill) you could send to your insurer yourself. Then compare the total with what you'd pay at an in-network clinic. Our [telehealth comparisons](/compare/) cover how each service handles insurance.

## Does insurance type affect who gets treated?

Yes. A 2026 study of nationally representative US survey data (NHANES, 2013–2020) found that women aged 45 to 64 with Medicaid used hormone therapy at about half the rate of women with private insurance [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]:

- Women with **Medicaid** reported ever using menopausal hormone therapy at **10.7%**, compared with **20.7%** for women with **private insurance** [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]
- After adjusting for other factors, Medicaid-insured women had about half the odds of hormone therapy use (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.87) [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]
- **Black women had lower odds of use than White women**, and this gap shrank, but did not close, once insurance was taken into account [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]

The authors say insurance may help explain gaps in menopause care, and call for efforts to remove insurance-related barriers [@chesnokova-insurance-mht-disparity-2026]. If you have Medicaid and your first try at care doesn't go well, it's worth persisting with a second opinion or a [menopause specialist](/care/find-a-menopause-specialist/).
