The main difference between a vaginal moisturiser and a lubricant is timing and purpose: a moisturiser is used regularly to keep dry tissues comfortable throughout the day, while a lubricant is used specifically during sexual activity to reduce friction, as outlined in our [menopause care directory](/care/) [@nams-2020-gsm]. Many women with menopause dryness need both [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm].

You can check your personal discomfort pattern across intimate and daily activities using our interactive [symptoms checklist](/tools/symptoms-checklist/).

## Side-by-side comparison

Understanding the role of each product helps you select the right comfort tool for your specific symptoms:

| Feature | Vaginal moisturiser | Personal lubricant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary purpose** | Continuous tissue hydration and soothing day-to-day dryness [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm] | Immediate reduction of friction and skin shear during intimacy [@nams-2020-gsm] |
| **When to apply** | Regularly (typically 2 to 3 times per week), independent of sexual activity | Immediately before and during sexual intercourse or pelvic touch |
| **How long it lasts** | Works for 2 to 3 days by adhering to the vaginal mucosa [@cochrane-vaginal-moisturizers] | Lasts during the intimate session; may need reapplication if water-based |
| **Application location** | Inside the vagina with an applicator, suppository, or finger | On the vulva, vaginal opening, partner, or pelvic toys |
| **What it cannot do** | Does not restore estrogen levels, rebuild elastic tissue, or prevent hot flashes | Does not provide lasting day-to-day hydration or relieve daytime irritation |

## When to choose a vaginal moisturiser

If you feel persistent dryness, itching, stinging, or soreness while sitting, walking, exercising, or going about your normal routine, you need a **vaginal moisturiser** [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm]. 

Moisturisers contain hydrophilic ingredients like polycarbophil or hyaluronic acid that bind water molecules to epithelial cells, gradually restoring tissue moisture over days [@cochrane-vaginal-moisturizers].

When selecting a moisturiser, look for:
- A pH between 3.8 and 4.5 to match healthy vaginal acidity [@nams-2020-gsm].
- Osmolality under 380 mOsm/kg to prevent drawing fluid out of already dry cells.
- Absence of parabens, fragrances, and warming additives that can trigger contact dermatitis.

Moisturisers come as gels used with an applicator and as small inserts (suppositories). Polycarbophil and hyaluronic acid products work in the same way, by holding water against the tissue [@cochrane-vaginal-moisturizers], so choose the form you find easiest to use on a regular schedule.

## When to choose a personal lubricant

If you feel comfortable throughout your workday but experience friction, chafing, or discomfort during intercourse or pelvic exams, you need a **personal lubricant** [@nams-2020-gsm].

Thinning postmenopausal tissue cannot withstand friction without adequate slip [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm]. Personal lubricants come in two primary types:
- **Silicone-based lubricants**: Hypoallergenic, extremely slick, and long-lasting because silicone does not evaporate or absorb into tissue. Ideal for sensitive postmenopausal mucosa, though not compatible with silicone toys.
- **Water-based lubricants**: Easy to rinse and safe with all devices, but may dry out faster and require reapplication. Always verify that water-based options are iso-osmotic and free of glycerine.

When choosing a lubricant, check the ingredient list for fragrance, warming or tingling agents, and flavourings, which can irritate thinning tissue, and check that it is condom-compatible if you use condoms.

## Can you use both together?

Yes. In fact, clinical guidelines frequently recommend a dual approach for women experiencing both daily dryness and intimacy discomfort [@nams-2020-gsm]. Using a moisturiser on a set schedule (such as Monday, Wednesday, and Friday) keeps baseline tissue hydrated, while applying silicone lubricant during sex ensures comfort [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm].

If you experience unexplained bleeding after sex or at any time after menopause, schedule an assessment with a doctor promptly [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding]. Postmenopausal bleeding must always be evaluated to rule out endometrial pathology.

## Questions people ask

### Does coconut oil work as a vaginal moisturiser?
While some women use organic oils, food-grade oils are not pH-balanced, can disrupt the vaginal microbiome, and degrade latex condoms [@acog-clinical-consensus-gsm]. Medical-grade moisturisers formulated for vaginal osmolality are the safer first-line choice.

### What if neither moisturisers nor lubricants are enough?
If over-the-counter options do not resolve discomfort after several weeks, ask your clinician about low-dose prescription vaginal estrogen [@nams-2020-gsm]. Prescription local estrogen directly stimulates tissue repair and natural lubrication with minimal systemic absorption. A nightly vaginal DHEA insert (prasterone) is another prescription option with real trial evidence — see our [DHEA evidence verdict](/does-it-work/dhea/) for how it compares.
