Black cohosh is a plant supplement often taken for hot flashes, but trials haven't shown it works better than a placebo pill [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. If you take it, stop and see a doctor if you get belly pain, dark urine or yellowing skin [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. Hormone therapy, CBT and some non-hormonal prescriptions have the strongest evidence [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

Black cohosh (*Actaea racemosa*, also called *Cimicifuga racemosa*) is the most purchased plant remedy for menopause symptoms [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. It is most often used for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/) [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. No one knows which of its ingredients might be active, or how it might work [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].

## What the best evidence found

- **Cochrane review (2012)**: 16 randomized trials with 2,027 women found no significant difference between black cohosh and placebo in how often hot flashes happened [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. The review's authors said there was "insufficient evidence" to either support or oppose its use [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **Two high-quality trials**: one with 351 women and one with 88 women compared black cohosh with a placebo for a year. Neither found that black cohosh reduced the number or intensity of hot flashes and night sweats more than placebo [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **The Menopause Society (2023)**: says there is insufficient evidence to support black cohosh for hot flashes and does not recommend it. Its rating, Level I, means the panel judged the evidence good and consistent [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## Why some people feel better taking it

In trials of non-hormone treatments for hot flashes, people taking a placebo improve too, by 20% to 66% [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Women with more anxiety tend to respond more to placebo [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. So feeling better after starting black cohosh doesn't show that the herb caused the change. That's why trials that compare it head to head with a matching placebo matter most.

## Products vary a lot

Black cohosh is sold as powdered herb, liquid extracts and dried extracts in pills, and these preparations vary considerably in their chemical makeup [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. Supplements also lack government regulation to ensure their purity and safety [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Two bottles with the same name may not contain the same thing.

## Doses used in studies

The trials in the Cochrane review used 8 to 160 mg a day of various black cohosh extracts, with a middle (median) dose of 40 mg a day [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. The two larger trials described above used 160 mg and 128 mg a day [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. These are the amounts studies used, not a recommended dose, and none of them beat placebo.

## Side effects, liver safety and interactions

- **Common side effects**: in trials, side effects were uncommon. The ones reported most often were mild, short-lived stomach upset and rashes [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **Long-term safety**: most studies lasted 6 months or less, so long-term safety hasn't been studied [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **Liver injury reports**: at least 83 cases of liver damage, including liver inflammation (hepatitis) and liver failure, have been reported worldwide in people using black cohosh. No one has proven that black cohosh caused them. Some cases may have come from contaminated products or the wrong plant species, though this was never confirmed by testing [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020]. A US Pharmacopeia expert committee found 30 reports possibly related to black cohosh [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
- **Warning labels**: the US Pharmacopeia recommends a liver warning on black cohosh labels, but the FDA does not require one, so a product may not carry it [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **Medicine interactions**: black cohosh is not known to have clinically important interactions with medicines, but this hasn't been studied systematically [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].

## Who should avoid it

- **People with a liver disorder**: the US Pharmacopeia advises people with liver disorders to avoid black cohosh [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].
- **People who are pregnant**: the American Herbal Products Association advises not taking it during pregnancy except under a health care provider's supervision, because its use in pregnancy hasn't been well studied [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020].

## When to see a doctor

Tell your doctor or pharmacist about any supplement you take, including black cohosh, especially if you have liver problems or take other medicines.

:::urgent
**If you take black cohosh, stop it and contact a doctor if you notice any of these possible signs of liver trouble** [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]:

- Abdominal (belly) pain
- Dark urine
- Yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (jaundice)

If you feel very unwell, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.
:::

## What has better evidence instead

If you want to manage hot flashes without hormones, The Menopause Society recommends CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant (Level I), and oxybutynin (Levels I–II) [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]:
