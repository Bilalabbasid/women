**Our verdict: Not recommended as a treatment for hot flashes, based on lower-confidence evidence (Level III).**

Changing your diet — cutting certain foods, eating more plant-based meals, or following a specific eating pattern — is widely suggested as a way to reduce [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/). The Menopause Society's 2023 Nonhormone Therapy Position Statement reviewed this and placed **dietary modification** in the **not recommended** category for vasomotor symptoms, though at **Level III**, its lower-confidence evidence tier [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## What the Level III rating actually means

Level III is the weakest tier in this grading system, which means the case against dietary modification is less firmly established than, say, the Level I evidence against [paced breathing](/does-it-work/paced-breathing/). In practice: there just isn't strong trial evidence that any specific diet reliably reduces hot flash frequency or severity, one way or the other.

Soy specifically was assessed on its own, separately from general diet, and also landed in the **not recommended** category — see our dedicated [soy and isoflavones](/does-it-work/soy-isoflavones/) verdict for that evidence.

## What this doesn't mean

Diet quality still matters for weight management, cardiovascular health, and bone health during and after menopause — see our [weight gain](/symptoms/weight-gain/) page for what's actually linked to midlife weight changes. That's a separate, real benefit, distinct from the narrower question of whether diet reliably stops hot flashes, which the current evidence doesn't support.
