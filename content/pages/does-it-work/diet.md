**Our verdict:** :grade{id="diet-vms"}. No specific diet has been shown in strong trials to stop hot flashes, and The Menopause Society does not recommend diet changes as a treatment (Level III, its lowest-confidence tier). Losing weight may help, and hormone therapy is the most effective treatment [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Cutting certain foods, eating more plant-based meals and following a set eating plan are all widely suggested for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/). Below: what the main studies found, which foods may act as triggers, how weight loss fits in, and where diet does matter in midlife.

## Can changing your diet stop hot flashes?

Not reliably, based on current evidence. The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement reviewed the research and placed dietary modification in its "not recommended" group for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. That rating is Level III, the weakest tier in its grading system, which means the evidence is thin in both directions rather than firmly negative [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

Soy was assessed on its own and also landed in the not-recommended group, at Level II [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Our [soy and isoflavones](/does-it-work/soy-isoflavones/) verdict covers that evidence.

## What did the vegan diet and soybean studies find?

Two small trials from the same research group reported large drops in hot flashes, but their design limits what they can show. In both, postmenopausal women were randomly assigned to a low-fat vegan diet with half a cup (86 g) of cooked soybeans a day, or to keep eating as usual, for 12 weeks:

- **First trial (38 women)**: total hot flashes fell 79% on the diet and 49% without changes; 10 of 17 women on the diet became free of moderate-to-severe hot flashes [@barnard-wavs-vegan-soy-trial-2021].
- **Second trial (84 women)**: moderate-to-severe hot flashes fell 88% on the diet vs 34% without changes, and half of the women who finished the diet reported none at 12 weeks [@barnard-vegan-soy-vms-rct-2023].

Why this isn't enough to call the diet a proven treatment:

- **No placebo and no blinding**: women knew whether they had changed their diet, and there was no dummy diet to compare against. Hot flashes also fell in the groups that made no changes, by 49% (all hot flashes, first trial) and 34% (moderate-to-severe, second trial) [@barnard-wavs-vegan-soy-trial-2021; @barnard-vegan-soy-vms-rct-2023].
- **Small and short**: the trials had 38 and 84 women and lasted 12 weeks [@barnard-wavs-vegan-soy-trial-2021; @barnard-vegan-soy-vms-rct-2023].
- **Funding**: the studies were run by a nonprofit that promotes plant-based diets, and the lead author is its president [@barnard-vegan-soy-vms-rct-2023].

## Does losing weight help hot flashes?

It may, and it has more support than any single diet. The Menopause Society lists weight loss among its recommended options for hot flashes, at Levels II–III [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. In the Women's Health Initiative, a US trial of more than 17,000 women not on hormone therapy, women who lost 10% or more of their body weight in the first year were more likely to have hot flashes and night sweats go away than women whose weight stayed the same [@kroenke-whi-diet-weight-vms-2012]. The low-fat diet itself had only a small link to symptoms going away; large weight loss mattered more [@kroenke-whi-diet-weight-vms-2012].

Our [weight gain](/symptoms/weight-gain/) page covers why weight shifts in midlife.

## Which foods help hot flashes?

No food has been proven to prevent them. In one Australian study that followed about 6,000 women for 9 years, those who ate the most fruit, or followed a Mediterranean-style pattern, were somewhat less likely to report hot flashes and night sweats; a high-fat, high-sugar pattern was linked to more [@herber-gast-diet-patterns-vms-2013]. That study observed eating habits rather than testing a diet, so it can't show that the food caused the difference.

## Do spicy food, caffeine or alcohol trigger hot flashes?

They can for some people, but cutting them out hasn't been shown to work as a treatment. The NHS suggests avoiding or reducing triggers such as spicy food, caffeine, hot drinks, smoking and alcohol [@nhs-menopause-things-you-can-do]. The Menopause Society's 2023 review placed trigger avoidance in its not-recommended group at Level II [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Noting what seems to set off your own hot flashes is low-risk, as long as it doesn't delay treatment that works.

## Where diet does matter in midlife

A good diet still has real benefits beyond hot flashes:

- **Bones**: women aged 51 to 70 need 1,200 mg of calcium a day, and the body absorbs and keeps less calcium after menopause [@ods-calcium-consumer-2023]. See our [calcium](/does-it-work/calcium/) page.
- **Weight and heart**: the hormone change at menopause is linked to more body fat, especially around the middle, and extra midlife weight is tied to higher heart and metabolic risk [@davis-ims-weight-gain-menopause-2012]. Our [heart health after menopause](/postmenopause/heart-health/) guide covers this.

## What has stronger evidence for hot flashes

- **[Hormone therapy (HRT)](/treatments/hormone-therapy/)**: the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **[Non-hormonal options](/treatments/nonhormonal/)**: CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant are rated Level I [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
