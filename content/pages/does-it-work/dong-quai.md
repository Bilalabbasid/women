**Our verdict: Not supported by clinical trial evidence for hot flashes.**

Dong quai (*Angelica sinensis*), a root used in traditional Chinese medicine, is often marketed for menopausal symptoms under the assumption that it acts like a mild estrogen. A rigorous, double-blind trial tested that assumption directly — and found it didn't hold up.

## What the trial actually found

A 24-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial randomized **71 postmenopausal women** with elevated FSH and hot flashes to either dong quai or placebo [@hirata-dong-quai-trial-1997]. The researchers measured endometrial thickness by ultrasound, vaginal cell maturation (a marker of estrogen exposure), and menopausal symptoms using the Kupperman index and a hot flash diary.

The result: **no statistically significant difference between groups** on any of these measures — not endometrial thickness, not vaginal maturation, not hot flash count, not the overall symptom score [@hirata-dong-quai-trial-1997]. The researchers concluded that, used alone, dong quai does **not** produce estrogen-like effects and was no more helpful than placebo for menopausal symptoms [@hirata-dong-quai-trial-1997].

## Why "used alone" matters

This trial specifically tested dong quai by itself, not combined with other herbs. You'll sometimes see dong quai included in multi-herb blends marketed for menopause, and a negative result for dong quai alone doesn't automatically tell you how those combination products perform — but it does remove dong quai itself as a credible active ingredient to look for on a label, since this is the most rigorous test of it available.

## What to consider

- **No proven benefit** for hot flashes based on this trial
- **Possible blood-thinning effect**: dong quai is generally understood to have anticoagulant-like properties, which matters if you take blood thinners or have an upcoming procedure
