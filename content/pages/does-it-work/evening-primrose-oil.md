**Our verdict:** :grade{id="evening-primrose-vms"} for hot flashes. In small trials, evening primrose oil did not reliably cut how often hot flashes happen, though a few results hint at milder flashes or fewer night sweats [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024; @larki-epo-menopause-metaanalysis-2025; @kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021]. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## What is evening primrose oil?

Evening primrose oil comes from the seeds of the evening primrose plant. It contains omega-6 fats, including one called gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024]. It is sold as a supplement for menopause symptoms and several other conditions, but there isn't enough evidence to say whether it helps menopause symptoms [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024].

## Does evening primrose oil help hot flashes?

Probably not in any way you would reliably notice. Across the trials, people taking evening primrose oil did not have fewer hot flashes than people taking a placebo, and the two reviews that pooled the trials disagree on whether flashes got shorter or milder [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024; @larki-epo-menopause-metaanalysis-2025]. Both say better studies are needed.

| Study | What was tested | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 trial, 56 women | Two 500 mg capsules a day or a placebo for 6 weeks [@farzaneh-epo-hot-flashes-rct-2013] | Flashes were milder than with placebo, but not less frequent or shorter [@farzaneh-epo-hot-flashes-rct-2013] |
| 2021 trial | One 1,000 mg capsule twice a day or a placebo for 8 weeks [@kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021] | No difference in how often, how long or how strong hot flashes were [@kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021] |
| 2024 review of trials | Evening primrose oil vs. placebo or other treatments [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024] | Milder flashes, but no change in how often or how long; evidence too weak for firm conclusions [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024] |
| 2025 review, 6 trials, 450 women | Evening primrose oil vs. controls [@larki-epo-menopause-metaanalysis-2025] | No real difference in flashes per day or strength; flashes were shorter; evidence of moderate to low certainty [@larki-epo-menopause-metaanalysis-2025] |

The Menopause Society, which writes the main US guidance on non-hormone treatments, does not recommend over-the-counter supplements or herbal remedies for hot flashes (Levels I–II) [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## Does it help night sweats?

There is one encouraging but weak result. The 2021 trial measured [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/) separately and found they were less frequent and less severe with evening primrose oil than with a placebo [@kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021]. The trial was small and single-blind, which means the researchers knew who was taking what, and no one has repeated it yet.

## Does it help other menopause symptoms?

There isn't enough evidence to say it helps menopause symptoms in general [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024]. For breast pain, evening primrose oil is graded :grade{id="epo-mastalgia"}: it has not worked better than a placebo, and UK guidance doesn't recommend it routinely [@hubbard-breast-pain-assessment-2020]. See [breast tenderness](/symptoms/breast-tenderness/) for what does help.

## Does evening primrose oil help with menopause weight gain?

There's no evidence that it does. None of the menopause trials or reviews of evening primrose oil we found measured weight change: they looked at hot flashes, night sweats and other symptoms [@kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021; @farzaneh-epo-hot-flashes-rct-2013; @thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024; @larki-epo-menopause-metaanalysis-2025]. The NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health doesn't mention weight on its evening primrose oil page, and says there isn't enough evidence that it helps menopause symptoms [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024]. For what is known about weight in midlife, see [menopause weight gain](/symptoms/weight-gain/).

## How much did studies use, and how long does it take to work?

The trials used two 500 mg capsules a day for 6 weeks, or one 1,000 mg capsule twice a day for 8 weeks [@farzaneh-epo-hot-flashes-rct-2013; @kazemi-evening-primrose-oil-2021]. These are the amounts studied, not a recommended dose. No trial has shown how long it takes to work, and longer use hasn't been tested for menopause.

## Side effects and safety

Evening primrose oil is probably safe for most adults when taken by mouth [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024]. The usual side effects are:

- **Stomach upset**: Belly pain, indigestion, nausea, soft stools or diarrhea [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024; @mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].
- **Headache**: Reported in the trials and by Memorial Sloan Kettering [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024; @mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].

Memorial Sloan Kettering advises against it in pregnancy because of possible pregnancy problems, and says people with hormone-sensitive cancers should use it with caution [@mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].

## Drug interactions to ask about

Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking it if you take any medicine, because some herbs and medicines interact in harmful ways [@nccih-evening-primrose-oil-2024]. Memorial Sloan Kettering lists these:

- **Blood thinners and antiplatelet drugs**: Such as warfarin. Together they may raise the risk of bleeding [@mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].
- **Lopinavir (Kaletra) and other HIV drugs**: Evening primrose oil can raise the levels of these drugs in the blood [@mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].
- **Blood pressure**: No interaction with blood pressure drugs has been reported, but one population study linked it to higher blood pressure [@mskcc-evening-primrose-oil-2022].

## What has stronger evidence for hot flashes?

Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. If you can't or don't want to take hormones, The Menopause Society recommends CBT, clinical hypnosis, certain antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs), gabapentin and fezolinetant (Level I) [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
