The most effective form of exercise for women experiencing perimenopause and menopause is progressive resistance training (lifting challenging weights) combined with brief impact loading [@liftmor-trial-bone-density, @sims-next-level]. While moderate walking and cardio support heart health, they do not provide enough mechanical force to halt the accelerated loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia) and bone mineral density that follows the postmenopausal estrogen decline, as documented in our [Does It Work evidence directory](/does-it-work/) [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

## Why resistance and impact training matter in menopause

During your reproductive years, estrogen acts as an anabolic and anti-resorptive hormone, preserving muscle stem cells and preventing bone breakdown. When estrogen declines:
1. **Muscle loss accelerates**: Women lose up to 1% of muscle mass annually after age 40, leading to reduced resting metabolic rate, increased insulin resistance, and joint instability [@sims-next-level].
2. **Bone resorption outpaces formation**: Without estrogen's protective signal to osteoclasts, bone mineral density drops rapidly, particularly in the hip and lumbar spine [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

### What the clinical trials show

The landmark LIFTMOR randomized controlled trial evaluated twice-weekly, 30-minute supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT) in postmenopausal women with osteopenia and osteoporosis [@liftmor-trial-bone-density]. 

Over eight months, women lifting heavy weights (at 80–85% of their one-rep maximum across deadlifts, overhead presses, and squats) experienced significant increases in lumbar spine (+2.9%) and femoral neck (+1.5%) bone mineral density, alongside marked improvements in balance and functional performance, with zero vertebral fractures or major injuries [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

## A simple starter plan: 3 core movement patterns

You do not need to spend hours in the gym. Doing two to three focused 30-to-40-minute sessions per week that target the body's primary compound movement patterns delivers the mechanical stimulus needed [@sims-next-level]:

1. **The Squat (Knee-dominant lower body)**:
   - *Starter*: Bodyweight box squats (sitting down to a chair and standing up without using your hands).
   - *Progression*: Holding a single dumbbell or kettlebell at your chest (goblet squat).
   - *Dose*: 3 sets of 6 to 8 repetitions with challenging weight.

2. **The Hinge (Hip-dominant posterior chain)**:
   - *Starter*: Dumbbell Romanian deadlifts, pushing your hips backward while keeping your spine neutral.
   - *Progression*: Trap-bar or barbell deadlifts.
   - *Dose*: 3 sets of 5 to 8 repetitions.

3. **Upper Body Push & Pull**:
   - *Push*: Overhead dumbbell press or incline push-ups (3 sets of 8 reps).
   - *Pull*: Supported dumbbell rows or resistance band pulldowns (3 sets of 8 to 10 reps).

4. **Multi-directional impact (for bone stimulation)**:
   - 30 to 50 low-height hops, jumping jacks, or brisk heel drops daily to send piezo-electric mechanical signals through the femur and spine [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

## Safety precautions and progression

To train safely and avoid injury:
- **Master form before adding load**: Learn proper spinal alignment and hip hinging with light weights or bodyweight before increasing resistance.
- **Consult your clinician if you have osteoporosis**: If you have established severe osteoporosis or a history of fragility fractures, work with a physical therapist knowledgeable in the LIFTMOR protocol to avoid unsupported spinal flexion [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].
- **Prioritize protein recovery**: Postmenopausal women experience anabolic resistance; aim for 25 to 30 grams of high-quality protein per meal to trigger muscle protein synthesis [@sims-next-level].
