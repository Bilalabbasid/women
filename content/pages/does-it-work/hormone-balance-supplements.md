**Our verdict:** :grade{id="hormone-balance-supplements"}. "Hormone balance" supplements are blends of herbs, plant extracts and vitamins, and none has been shown to steady perimenopause hormone swings. The Menopause Society does not recommend over-the-counter supplements for hot flashes and night sweats (Levels I–II), and hormone therapy is the most effective treatment [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Ads for capsules, powders and gummies promise to "balance your hormones" during [perimenopause](/perimenopause/) and [menopause](/menopause/). In clinical medicine, "hormone imbalance" is a sales slogan rather than a diagnosis [@gunter-menopause-manifesto]. Below: what these blends usually contain, what studies found for each ingredient, the safety questions, and what to try instead.

## Do hormone balance supplements work?

No blend sold for "hormone balance" has good trial evidence behind it for menopause symptoms. The Menopause Society's 2023 review placed over-the-counter supplements and herbal remedies in its "not recommended" group for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. An NIH review reached a similar view: plant estrogens, herbs and other supplements haven't been clearly shown to relieve menopause symptoms [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017].

The trials in the table further down each tested one ingredient. We haven't found trials of the multi-ingredient blends themselves, so their mix of ingredients and amounts is untested as a whole.

## Why can't a supplement "balance" perimenopause hormones?

Because the swings come from the ovaries winding down, not from something missing that a pill can top up. In the late menopause transition, cycle length varies more, hormone levels swing widely, and more cycles pass without an egg being released [@straw-10-2012]. Herbal blends, whether they contain [black cohosh](/does-it-work/black-cohosh/), [soy isoflavones](/does-it-work/soy-isoflavones/), ashwagandha or B vitamins, don't restart regular ovarian cycles [@gunter-menopause-manifesto; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## Can a test show whether my hormones are "out of balance"?

Usually not, and most people don't need one. For otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over, UK NICE guidance says perimenopause is recognized from symptoms and period changes, and advises against using hormone tests such as estradiol or AMH to identify it [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Because levels swing so much in the transition, one result is only a snapshot [@straw-10-2012]. Our guide to [perimenopause blood tests](/perimenopause/blood-tests/) explains when a test can still help.

## What's in hormone balance supplements, and what did studies find?

Labels vary from brand to brand, but these ingredients turn up often. Here is the evidence for each one on its own:

| Ingredient | What studies found | Safety note |
|---|---|---|
| [Black cohosh](/does-it-work/black-cohosh/) | Two high-quality trials found it no better than placebo for hot flashes and night sweats [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020] | Rare reports of liver damage; people with liver disorders should avoid it [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020] |
| [Soy isoflavones](/does-it-work/soy-isoflavones/) | Results are inconsistent; not recommended for hot flashes (Level II) [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017] | May not be safe for women who shouldn't take estrogen [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017] |
| [Dong quai](/does-it-work/dong-quai/) | No better than placebo for hot flashes in a 24-week trial of 71 women [@hirata-dong-quai-trial-1997] | May interact with the blood thinner warfarin [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017] |
| [Evening primrose oil](/does-it-work/evening-primrose-oil/) | A 2024 review found no difference from placebo in how often hot flashes happened [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024] | Mild nausea and headache reported [@thevi-epo-hot-flashes-metaanalysis-2024] |
| [Ashwagandha](/does-it-work/ashwagandha/) | One 8-week trial of 100 perimenopausal women in India reported lower symptom scores than placebo [@gopal-ashwagandha-perimenopause-2021] | Case reports of liver injury; may raise thyroid hormone levels [@ods-ashwagandha-hp-2025] |
| [Maca](/does-it-work/maca/) | Four small trials leaned favorable, but reviewers called the evidence limited [@lee-maca-menopause-systematic-review-2011] | Safety has not been proven [@lee-maca-menopause-systematic-review-2011] |

Even the two ingredients with some positive results, ashwagandha and maca, rest on small, short studies of single ingredients, not on the blends sold as "hormone balance" products.

## Are hormone balance supplements safe?

They have not been shown to be safe for long-term use. Supplements for menopause may cause side effects or interact with medicines, and little is known about their safety over years [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017]. Points to know:

- **No approval before sale**: FDA does not approve dietary supplements or their labels before they are sold; the maker is responsible for safety. A product that claims to treat a disease is regulated as a drug [@fda-dietary-supplements-qa-2026].
- **Liver problems**: black cohosh and ashwagandha have case reports of liver injury, and kava has been linked to severe liver disease [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020; @ods-ashwagandha-hp-2025; @nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017].
- **Medicine interactions**: dong quai may interact with warfarin, and ashwagandha might interact with thyroid, diabetes and blood pressure medicines [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017; @ods-ashwagandha-hp-2025].
- **Estrogen-like ingredients**: guidelines cited by the NIH caution that women with a personal or strong family history of breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, blood clots or heart disease should not use soy-based treatments [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017]. See our page for [breast cancer survivors](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/).
- **Label words**: "natural" doesn't always mean safe, and words like "standardized" or "certified" don't by themselves mean consistent quality [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017].

## When to see a doctor

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every supplement you take, especially if you take prescription medicines, have liver disease, or have had a hormone-sensitive cancer [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017]. If hot flashes, sleep problems or mood changes are affecting daily life, a doctor can go through treatments with stronger evidence.

:::urgent
**Stop the supplement and contact a doctor promptly** if you develop stomach pain, dark urine, or yellowing of your skin or the whites of your eyes (jaundice) while taking a product that contains black cohosh or ashwagandha. These can be signs of liver injury [@nih-ods-black-cohosh-2020; @ods-ashwagandha-hp-2025].
:::

## What has real clinical trial evidence instead

These options are backed by randomized trials and guidelines:

- **[Menopausal hormone therapy (HRT)](/treatments/hormone-therapy/)**: the most effective treatment for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/), and it also prevents bone loss [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **[Non-hormonal treatments](/treatments/nonhormonal/)**: CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and [fezolinetant](/treatments/nonhormonal/fezolinetant/) are rated Level I by The Menopause Society [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Our [elinzanetant](/treatments/nonhormonal/elinzanetant/) page covers a newer prescription option.
- **Prepare for your appointment**: the [does it work? evidence checker](/tools/does-it-work-checker/) and [treatment conversation builder](/tools/treatment-questions/) help you compare options before you go.
