**Our verdict:** :grade{id="paced-breathing-vms"}. In trials, slow, paced breathing did not reduce hot flashes more than ordinary breathing did, and The Menopause Society does not recommend it as a hot flash treatment [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. No treatment eases hot flashes more than hormone therapy [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## What is paced breathing?

It is slow, deep breathing from the belly, done at a steady, unhurried rate. It belongs to the same family as other relaxation methods, which aim to calm the body's stress response [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021]. It is often suggested as a self-help step for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/).

The trials taught it from a CD or audio guide. One asked women to breathe at 6 breaths a minute for 15 minutes once or twice a day [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013]. Another also asked them to start slow breathing at the first sign of each hot flash [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013].

## Does paced breathing help hot flashes?

No, not by enough to matter in well-run trials. The Menopause Society placed paced breathing in its "not recommended" group for hot flashes and night sweats in 2023, at Level I [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Level I means the panel judged the evidence good and consistent, so this isn't a case of "nobody has studied it yet."

Some experts read "not recommended" as "not a first choice" rather than "proven useless" [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Either way, it isn't a reliable way to cut hot flashes.

## What did the studies find?

| Study | Breathing compared with | Result |
|---|---|---|
| US trial, 2013: 218 women, including 96 breast cancer survivors [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013] | 16 weeks of paced breathing vs. fast, shallow breathing vs. usual care [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013] | No difference in how often hot flashes came, how bad they were or how much they bothered women. Most didn't halve their hot flashes, even when doing the breathing correctly every day [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013] |
| Mayo Clinic trial, 2013: 92 women [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013] | 9 weeks of paced breathing once or twice a day vs. normal-paced breathing [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013] | Hot flashes fell 52% (twice a day), 42% (once a day) and 46% (normal breathing) [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013] |
| Review, 2017: pooled trials of paced breathing [@goldstein-mindfulness-relaxation-vms-2017] | Paced breathing vs. control groups [@goldstein-mindfulness-relaxation-vms-2017] | No real drop in how often hot flashes happened or how severe they were [@goldstein-mindfulness-relaxation-vms-2017] |

Not every review agrees. The NIH's complementary health center describes a 2019 review of 4 studies with 398 people that reported paced breathing improved hot flashes [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021]. The larger controlled trial and The Menopause Society's review point the other way [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## Why does it seem to work for some people?

Because hot flashes can ease over a few weeks even without the technique. In the Mayo Clinic trial, women who simply breathed at a normal pace had a 46% drop, close to the 42% to 52% seen with paced breathing [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013]. Without a comparison group, that natural improvement is easy to credit to the breathing. Most women in that trial said the exercises were easy to do [@sood-paced-breathing-trial-2013].

## Can paced breathing help with stress or sleep?

Possibly with stress, less so with sleep. NIH reviewers describe early evidence that slow belly breathing may reduce stress [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021]. Relaxation methods on their own don't look especially promising for sleep, though they are often one part of CBT for insomnia [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021]. In the 16-week trial, small changes in mood and sleep were as likely to favor the fast-breathing group as the paced-breathing group [@carpenter-paced-respiration-rct-2013].

## Is paced breathing safe?

For most healthy people, yes. Relaxation methods are generally considered safe, but a few people feel more anxious, have unwanted thoughts or fear losing control while doing them [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021]. Rarely, they may bring on or worsen symptoms in people with epilepsy, some mental health conditions, or a history of abuse or trauma [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021].

| Option | Grade for hot flashes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [Hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) | :grade{id="mht-vms"} | The most effective treatment, for women who can take it [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] |
| [CBT and clinical hypnosis](/treatments/nonhormonal/cbt-and-hypnosis/) | :grade{id="cbt-hypnosis-vms"} | Drug-free options with Level I support [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |
| [Non-hormonal prescriptions](/treatments/nonhormonal/) | :grade{id="nonhormonal-rx-vms"} | SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant, Level I [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |

CBT and hypnosis are the closest relatives of paced breathing: they are also drug-free, but they have the trial support that breathing alone lacks [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. If you've had breast cancer, read [breast cancer survivors and menopause](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/), and compare all graded options in the [does it work? evidence checker](/tools/does-it-work-checker/).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if hot flashes or night sweats are breaking your sleep or getting in the way of work or daily life, because effective treatments exist [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. If you were using breathing exercises to manage anxiety, and the anxiety is severe or long-lasting, get help from a health professional [@nccih-relaxation-techniques-2021].
