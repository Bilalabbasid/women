**Our verdict: Not supported once placebo is properly controlled for.**

Relizen is a pollen-extract supplement marketed for hot flashes and night sweats. It belongs to a product family (also sold as Femal and Serelys in other markets) that has actually been studied more than most menopause supplements — which makes it a useful, concrete example of why controlled trials matter.

## What the evidence actually shows

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis focused specifically on **purified pollen extract** — explicitly searching for Relizen, Femal, and Serelys by name — for vasomotor symptoms [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. It identified 5 studies (1 randomized controlled trial and 4 observational studies, 420 women total). Pooling **all** of them together showed significant improvements in hot flashes, night disturbance, depression, and muscle aches.

But here's the critical finding: when the reviewers limited their analysis to **only the placebo-controlled studies**, those improvements **were no longer statistically significant** [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. Their own conclusion states plainly that the vasomotor symptom improvements seen in uncontrolled studies "may have been due to the placebo effect" [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. The good news in the same review: no significant adverse effects were associated with pollen extract use.

## A closer look at Relizen's own marketing

Relizen's own product page cites a **75% reduction in hot flash frequency** and a **70% reduction in night sweats**, presented under a "Results backed by science" heading [@relizen-own-site-2026]. Looking at the brand's own footnote for that specific figure, the source is a **survey of 4,925 customers who had already been taking Relizen for at least 3 months** — not a placebo-controlled clinical trial [@relizen-own-site-2026].

That distinction matters a lot. A survey of people who were already loyal, paying customers after three months will systematically miss anyone who stopped because it didn't work, and can't separate a real effect from the placebo response that the independent meta-analysis found disappears under proper controls. Presenting a customer-satisfaction survey figure under a "science" heading, next to citations of actual clinical trials, blurs a distinction that matters for an honest evaluation.

## What to consider

- **No proven benefit over placebo** once controlled trials are isolated, per the most recent independent meta-analysis
- **No significant safety concerns** were identified — this isn't a safety warning, just an efficacy one
