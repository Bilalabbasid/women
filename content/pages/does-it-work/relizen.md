**Our verdict:** :grade{id="relizen-vms"}. Relizen's pollen-extract ingredient looked helpful in uncontrolled studies, but not once the analysis was limited to controlled studies, and reviewers say the benefit may have been a placebo effect [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Relizen is a pollen-extract supplement marketed for hot flashes and night sweats [@relizen-own-site-2026]. The same type of ingredient is sold under other names, such as Femal and Serelys [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. That makes it a useful, concrete example of why controlled trials matter.

## What the evidence actually shows

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis looked at **purified pollen extract** for hot flashes and night sweats. Its search terms included the brand names Bonafide (the company that sells Relizen), Femal, Estroven and Serelys [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024; @relizen-own-site-2026]. It found 5 studies (1 randomized controlled trial and 4 observational studies, 420 women in total). Pooling **all** of them showed significant improvements in hot flashes, night-time disturbance, depression and muscle aches [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024].

But here's the critical finding: when the reviewers pooled **only the controlled studies**, those improvements **were no longer statistically significant** [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. Their conclusion says the improvements in hot flashes seen in uncontrolled studies "may have been due to the placebo effect" [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. The good news in the same review: pollen extract was not linked to any significant side effects [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024].

## A closer look at Relizen's own marketing

Relizen's own product page says **75% of women had fewer hot flashes** and **70% had fewer night sweats**, under a "Results backed by science" heading [@relizen-own-site-2026]. Looking at the brand's own footnote for that specific figure, the source is a **survey of 4,925 customers who had already been taking Relizen for at least 3 months** — not a placebo-controlled clinical trial [@relizen-own-site-2026].

That distinction matters a lot. A survey of people who were already loyal, paying customers after three months will systematically miss anyone who stopped because it didn't work, and can't separate a real effect from the placebo response that the independent meta-analysis found disappears under proper controls. Presenting a customer-satisfaction survey figure under a "science" heading, next to citations of actual clinical trials, blurs a distinction that matters for an honest evaluation.

## What to consider

- **No proven benefit over placebo**: Once only the controlled studies were pooled, the benefit was no longer significant [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024].
- **Safety**: The review found no significant side effects, so this is a question of whether it works, not a safety warning [@acquarulo-pollen-extract-vms-metaanalysis-2024]. We haven't found a source on drug interactions or on who should avoid it, so ask your pharmacist if you take other medicines.
