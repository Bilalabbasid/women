**Our verdict:** :grade{id="soy-vms"}. Soy isoflavone pills may reduce hot flashes a little, but results across trials are inconsistent, and The Menopause Society does not recommend soy foods, soy extracts or equol as a hot flash treatment (Level II). Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Soybeans contain plant compounds called **isoflavones** (such as genistein and daidzein) that are similar in structure to estrogen [@nccih-soy-2025]. Some people's gut bacteria turn daidzein into **S-equol**, which is also sold as a supplement. Below: what the trials found, the doses studied, safety, breast cancer questions, and better-proven options for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/).

## Do soy isoflavones help hot flashes?

They might help a little, but not reliably enough for guidelines to recommend them. The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement reviewed the trials and placed soy foods, soy extracts and equol in its "not recommended" group for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. An NIH fact sheet puts it this way: soy may reduce how often and how badly hot flashes happen, but the effect may be small and results haven't been fully consistent [@nccih-soy-2025].

## What do the big reviews show?

Two well-known reviews pooled the trials and reached different-sounding conclusions:

- **2012 meta-analysis (17 trials)**: soy isoflavone supplements cut hot flash frequency by 20.6% and severity by 26.2% more than placebo. But results varied a lot from trial to trial, and the authors called for more research on dose, form and duration [@taku-soy-isoflavones-hot-flash-metaanalysis-2012].
- **2013 Cochrane review (43 trials, 4,364 women)**: looked at all plant estrogens, including soy and red clover, and found no conclusive evidence that they reduce hot flashes or night sweats [@lethaby-cochrane-phytoestrogens-2013].

The Cochrane review also found a strong placebo effect: hot flashes fell by 1% to 59% in women taking dummy pills [@lethaby-cochrane-phytoestrogens-2013]. That helps explain why soy can feel effective in everyday use even when trials show only a small extra benefit. See our [red clover](/does-it-work/red-clover/) verdict for how that extract fared in the same review.

## What doses of soy isoflavones were studied, and how long do they take?

Trials used a range of amounts, and none has been shown to be a reliable dose. In the 2012 meta-analysis, women took a median of 54 mg of isoflavones a day for 6 weeks to 12 months [@taku-soy-isoflavones-hot-flash-metaanalysis-2012]. Products with more than 18.8 mg of genistein, one of the main isoflavones, worked about twice as well on frequency as lower-genistein products [@taku-soy-isoflavones-hot-flash-metaanalysis-2012]. The Cochrane review likewise noted that extracts with over 30 mg a day of genistein gave more consistent results in four trials, and said they need more study [@lethaby-cochrane-phytoestrogens-2013]. These are amounts studies used, not a recommendation.

## Is soy safe? Side effects and interactions

In research studies, concentrated isoflavone extracts have been used safely for up to 6 months, and soy protein supplements for up to 16 weeks [@nccih-soy-2025]. Long-term safety of the extracts hasn't been established [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017]. Things to know:

- **Side effects**: bloating, constipation, diarrhea and nausea [@nccih-soy-2025].
- **Allergy**: some people are allergic to soy, and US labels must say when a product contains it [@nccih-soy-2025].
- **Antidepressant interaction**: soy foods high in tyramine, such as tofu and soy sauce, have caused dangerously high blood pressure in people taking MAOI antidepressants [@nccih-soy-2025].
- **Estrogen-like effects**: because isoflavones may act like estrogen, the supplements may not be safe for women who have been told not to take estrogen [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017].
- **Endometrium**: the Cochrane review found no evidence that the extracts stimulated the lining of the womb or vagina in studies lasting up to 2 years [@lethaby-cochrane-phytoestrogens-2013].

## Is soy safe if you've had breast cancer?

Soy foods appear to be safe; concentrated supplements are less clear. The American Cancer Society says eating soy foods does not seem to raise the risk of breast cancer coming back, and some studies suggest it may even lower it [@acs-breast-cancer-recurrence-diet-2026]. Supplements often contain much higher isoflavone levels, so it advises talking to your care team before taking them [@acs-breast-cancer-recurrence-diet-2026]. Guidelines cited by the NIH caution against soy-based treatments for women with a personal or strong family history of breast, uterine or ovarian cancer [@nccih-menopausal-symptoms-in-depth-2017]. Our [breast cancer survivors](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/) page covers hot flash options after cancer.

## Soy foods vs soy pills

Eating tofu, tempeh, edamame or soy milk as part of your meals is a different thing from taking concentrated isoflavone capsules expecting prescription-level relief. Soy supplements have not been shown to lower breast cancer risk, while higher soy intake from food is linked to lower risk among Asian and Asian-American women in observational studies [@nccih-soy-2025]. Soy isoflavones probably also help bone density after menopause [@nccih-soy-2025].

## What about S-equol supplements?

S-equol is the compound some people's gut bacteria make from soy. In one 12-week trial of 160 Japanese women whose bodies don't make equol, 10 mg a day reduced hot flash frequency more than placebo (59% vs 35% from baseline) [@aso-s-equol-hot-flash-trial-2012]. It's one trial in one group of women, and equol was included in The Menopause Society's not-recommended rating [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. See our [Equelle](/does-it-work/equelle/) page.

## Proven options to compare instead

- **[Menopausal hormone therapy (HRT)](/treatments/hormone-therapy/)**: the most effective option for moderate-to-severe hot flashes in women who can take it [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **[Non-hormonal treatments](/treatments/nonhormonal/)**: CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant are rated Level I [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
