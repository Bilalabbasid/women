**Our verdict: Not recommended at typical low doses for fracture prevention, and not currently recommended for fall prevention either; evidence is insufficient (not proven, not disproven) at higher doses for fractures.**

Vitamin D is widely recommended for bone health after [menopause](/menopause/), when bone loss accelerates (see our [bone health](/postmenopause/bone-health/) guide). But the most authoritative US guideline on this exact question — the **US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF)** — draws a more careful distinction than most marketing suggests.

## What the USPSTF actually concludes

For **postmenopausal women**, the USPSTF [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018]:

- **Recommends against** daily supplementation with **400 IU or less** of vitamin D (combined with 1,000mg or less of calcium) for preventing fractures — a **Grade D** recommendation, reflecting moderate certainty that this provides **no net benefit**.
- Concludes the evidence is **insufficient** (Grade I — not proven, not disproven) to judge the balance of benefits and harms at **higher doses**.
- Found **insufficient evidence** to make any recommendation for **men or premenopausal women**, at any dose.

In plain terms: the low doses commonly found in standard multivitamins and supplements don't show a net fracture-prevention benefit in the research reviewed, and the picture at higher doses simply isn't settled yet — not "proven to work," just "not yet answered." The USPSTF itself notes this topic is under active update, so it's worth checking for a newer statement before treating this as the final word.

## What about falls specifically?

This is worth being precise about, because guidance on this has changed over time. The USPSTF's **current** position is that it recommends **against** vitamin D supplementation for **fall prevention** in community-dwelling adults 65 and older [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018]. An earlier USPSTF statement (from around 2012) had recommended it for this purpose, but that position has since been superseded — if you've seen "vitamin D prevents falls" cited as current guidance, it's likely referencing that outdated statement.

## What this doesn't mean

This verdict is about *supplementing* vitamin D specifically to prevent fractures. It doesn't address correcting an actual, blood-test-confirmed vitamin D deficiency, which is a different clinical situation with its own, separate rationale for treatment. It's worth discussing your personal vitamin D level and fall risk with your clinician rather than applying this verdict as a blanket rule.
