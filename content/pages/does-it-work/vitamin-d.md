**Our verdict:** :grade{id="vitamin-d-fractures"} for preventing fractures at the low doses most studied. Vitamin D also doesn't ease hot flashes: in a large trial, it made no difference to them [@leblanc-whi-calcium-vitamin-d-menopause-symptoms-2015]. The recommended amount for women is 600 IU a day up to age 70 and 800 IU after that [@medlineplus-vitamin-d].

## Does vitamin D help hot flashes?

No, not in the largest trial that has tested it. In the Women's Health Initiative, about 34,000 women past menopause took 1,000 mg of calcium plus 400 IU of vitamin D a day, or a placebo, for an average of 5.7 years [@leblanc-whi-calcium-vitamin-d-menopause-symptoms-2015]. The two groups had the same number of menopause symptoms, including [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and night sweats [@leblanc-whi-calcium-vitamin-d-menopause-symptoms-2015].

Sleep problems, emotional well-being and energy didn't differ either [@leblanc-whi-calcium-vitamin-d-menopause-symptoms-2015]. That trial used 400 IU a day, and we haven't found a large trial showing that higher doses help hot flashes. For treatments that do work, see our [non-hormonal treatments](/treatments/nonhormonal/) guide.

## Does vitamin D protect your bones after menopause?

Not at the low doses most people take, when the goal is preventing fractures. For postmenopausal women, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018]:

- **Recommends against** daily supplements of **400 IU or less** of vitamin D (with 1,000 mg or less of calcium) to prevent fractures, a **Grade D** recommendation that reflects moderate certainty of **no net benefit**.
- Says the evidence is **insufficient** (Grade I, meaning not proven and not disproven) to judge **higher doses**.
- Found too little evidence to make any recommendation for **men or premenopausal women**, at any dose.

Researchers from the Women's Health Initiative reached a similar view: their results don't support giving calcium plus vitamin D to every postmenopausal woman to prevent fractures, but these nutrients are appropriate for women whose diet falls short of recommended amounts [@manson-whi-trials-clinical-practice-2024]. The USPSTF notes this topic is being updated, so check for a newer statement [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018].

Hormone therapy prevents bone loss and fractures [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy], and supervised high-intensity strength and impact training improved bone density in a trial of women past menopause [@liftmor-trial-bone-density]. See [bone health after menopause](/postmenopause/bone-health/) and our [strength training](/does-it-work/exercise/) verdict.

### Does vitamin D prevent falls?

No. The USPSTF currently recommends **against** vitamin D supplements to prevent falls in adults 65 and older living in the community [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018]. An older USPSTF statement, from around 2012, had recommended it, so if you've seen "vitamin D prevents falls" given as current advice, it is likely based on that outdated statement.

## How much vitamin D do you need after 50?

The recommended amounts below come from food, sun and supplements combined. They are general amounts for healthy people, not a personal dose:

| Age | Recommended a day | Upper limit a day |
|---|---|---|
| 19 to 70 | 600 IU (15 mcg) [@medlineplus-vitamin-d] | 4,000 IU (100 mcg) [@medlineplus-vitamin-d] |
| Over 70 | 800 IU (20 mcg) [@medlineplus-vitamin-d] | 4,000 IU (100 mcg) [@medlineplus-vitamin-d] |

Good food sources include fatty fish such as salmon, tuna and mackerel, plus fortified milk and cereals and mushrooms grown under UV light; your skin also makes vitamin D in sunlight [@medlineplus-vitamin-d]. A doctor may suggest more if you have risk factors for osteoporosis or low blood levels [@medlineplus-vitamin-d].

The Endocrine Society suggests healthy adults under 75 don't take more than these recommended amounts to try to prevent disease [@demay-endocrine-society-vitamin-d-2024]. It does suggest vitamin D for adults 75 and older. When vitamin D is needed after 50, it suggests a daily dose rather than occasional large doses [@demay-endocrine-society-vitamin-d-2024].

## How much is too much? The safe upper limit

For adults, the upper limit is 4,000 IU (100 mcg) a day [@medlineplus-vitamin-d]. Too much vitamin D almost always comes from taking too many supplements, not from food or sun [@medlineplus-vitamin-d]. It raises calcium in the blood, which can cause:

- **Stomach and appetite problems**: Nausea, vomiting, constipation, poor appetite and weight loss [@medlineplus-vitamin-d].
- **Kidney problems**: Kidney stones or kidney damage [@medlineplus-vitamin-d].
- **Other effects**: Confusion, weakness, and calcium deposits in the heart and lungs [@medlineplus-vitamin-d].

When adding up your intake, count every product you take, including any multivitamin.

## Should you get your vitamin D level tested?

Not routinely, if you have no symptoms. The USPSTF found too little evidence to say whether screening adults without symptoms does more good than harm [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021], and the Endocrine Society suggests against routine vitamin D blood tests [@demay-endocrine-society-vitamin-d-2024]. There is also no agreed blood level that defines deficiency [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021].

That guidance doesn't cover people with signs of deficiency, such as bone pain or muscle weakness, or people with a condition treated with vitamin D [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021]. Ask your doctor about testing in those cases. Lower vitamin D levels are more common with:

- **Little sun**: Winter, living far from the equator, or avoiding the sun [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021].
- **Diet**: Eating few foods with vitamin D [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021].
- **Age and weight**: Older age and obesity [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021].
- **Darker skin**: Being non-Hispanic Black, likely linked to skin pigment [@uspstf-vitamin-d-deficiency-screening-2021].

## Should you take vitamin D with calcium?

The fracture evidence above mostly tested the two together, and low doses of both did not prevent fractures [@uspstf-vitamin-d-calcium-fractures-2018]. Both are appropriate if your diet doesn't give you the recommended amounts [@manson-whi-trials-clinical-practice-2024]. See our companion verdict on [calcium after menopause](/does-it-work/calcium/).

## What this verdict doesn't mean

This verdict is about taking vitamin D to prevent fractures and falls, and to ease menopause symptoms. It doesn't address treating a deficiency found on a blood test, which is a separate medical question.
