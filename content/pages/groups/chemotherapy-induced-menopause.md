Being told that your cancer treatment might also end your fertility and trigger menopause — on top of everything else a cancer diagnosis brings — is a lot to take in at once. Here's what the evidence actually says about risk, reversibility, and what's worth asking before treatment begins.

## How likely is this, really?

It depends heavily on **which drugs** you're receiving and **your age**. A systematic review of 45 studies covering 5,607 female survivors of childhood and young adult cancer found that amenorrhoea (absent periods) rates after chemotherapy ranged from **0% to 83%**, depending on the specific treatment regimen [@overbeek-chemotherapy-ovarian-function-2017]. Some figures from that review:

- Patients on **MVPP protocols** (used for some lymphomas): **39–79%** developed amenorrhoea [@overbeek-chemotherapy-ovarian-function-2017]
- **Breast cancer survivors on cyclophosphamide-containing regimens**: **40–80%** developed amenorrhoea [@overbeek-chemotherapy-ovarian-function-2017]
- The strongest risk factors identified were **alkylating agents** (specifically procarbazine and busulfan) and **older age at the time of treatment** [@overbeek-chemotherapy-ovarian-function-2017]

The review's own authors are candid that all the studies they reviewed had methodological limitations, and call for better long-term tracking of cancer survivors [@overbeek-chemotherapy-ovarian-function-2017]. That's worth knowing: the exact numbers for your specific regimen may be better discussed directly with your oncologist, who can factor in your specific drugs, doses, and age.

## Temporary or permanent?

This genuinely varies. Younger women and those on lower-risk regimens are more likely to see their periods and ovarian function return in the months to a couple of years after finishing treatment. Older age and higher-risk regimens (especially alkylating agents) make permanent ovarian failure more likely. Because no one can predict this with certainty for an individual in advance, two things matter most: discussing **fertility preservation before treatment starts** if that's relevant to you, and **not assuming periods are gone for good** just because they stop during treatment.

## Fertility preservation: a before-treatment conversation

If future fertility matters to you, this is a conversation to have **before chemotherapy begins**, ideally with a referral to a fertility specialist alongside your oncology team. Standard options discussed at this stage typically include egg or embryo freezing and, in some cases, ovarian tissue preservation — which option (if any) fits depends on your specific cancer, treatment timeline, and how much time is available before treatment must start. This is time-sensitive, so raise it as early as possible rather than waiting.

## Managing symptoms if menopause does occur

If chemotherapy does trigger menopause symptoms — hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness — management needs to be coordinated with your oncology team, since systemic hormone therapy is often not an option depending on your cancer type. See our dedicated guide on [menopause after breast cancer](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/) for evidence-based non-hormonal options, many of which apply regardless of cancer type, and check any specific non-hormonal option against our [does it work? evidence directory](/does-it-work/) before trying it, since some supplements can interact with cancer treatment. If your periods don't return and you're under 40, see our guide on [premature ovarian insufficiency](/groups/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/) for what longer-term bone and heart protection involves.
