A history of endometriosis changes some important details about how menopause is managed — most notably, the type of hormone therapy that's actually safest for you. This isn't a minor technicality: getting it wrong can mean an unnecessary risk of reactivating the condition.

## The most important thing to know: estrogen-only therapy isn't automatically safe after hysterectomy

For most women, a hysterectomy (uterus removed) means estrogen-only hormone therapy is an option, because there's no uterus lining to protect from unopposed estrogen's endometrial cancer risk. **This general rule doesn't straightforwardly apply if you have a history of endometriosis.**

A 2025 clinical guide from the European Menopause and Andropause Society (EMAS) states that **estrogen-only MHT should be avoided, even for women who have had a hysterectomy**, if they have a history of endometriosis [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025]. The reasoning: endometriosis tissue can remain in the pelvis even after the uterus is removed, and estrogen alone can reactivate it or, rarely, drive malignant transformation. Instead, the guide recommends **continuous combined MHT** (estrogen plus a progestogen) as the safer regimen for both hysterectomized and non-hysterectomized women with this history, since the added progestogen may reduce the risk of recurrence and malignant transformation of any residual tissue [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].

## What else the guide covers

- **Endometriosis itself can bring menopause earlier.** The guide notes that endometriosis, or its treatment, may lead to premature or early menopause — worth knowing if you're younger than the typical menopause age and have this history [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].
- **Related health risks worth monitoring.** Endometriosis is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, ovarian, breast, and thyroid cancers, and osteoporosis [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025] — reasons to stay engaged with regular care rather than reasons to panic.
- **Surgery is the preferred option for symptomatic postmenopausal endometriosis.** If pain or other symptoms persist or return after menopause, the guide recommends surgery as the preferred management, since it reduces pain, confirms an accurate diagnosis, and lowers malignancy risk [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].
- **If symptoms return while on hormone therapy**, the guide outlines several options: adjusting the regimen, stopping hormone therapy, or switching to non-hormonal strategies [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].

## If hormone therapy isn't right for you

For vasomotor symptoms without hormone therapy, the guide points to **neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists** (the class that includes [fezolinetant](/treatments/nonhormonal/fezolinetant/)) as an option, alongside **antiresorptive or anabolic therapies plus calcium and vitamin D** specifically for bone protection [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].

One notable caution: the guide explicitly advises **against herbal preparations**, because their efficacy is uncertain and some contain estrogenic compounds — which matters more for you than for the general population, given the estrogen-sensitivity of residual endometriosis tissue [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025]. Check any specific supplement against our [does it work? evidence directory](/does-it-work/) before trying it, since several marketed menopause supplements are soy- or botanical-estrogen sources.

Logging any symptoms that return — pain, bleeding, or vasomotor symptoms — in our [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) can help your clinician judge whether it's a recurrence worth investigating.
