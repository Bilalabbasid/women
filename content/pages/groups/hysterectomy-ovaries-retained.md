If your uterus was removed but one or both ovaries stayed in place, you won't go through menopause right away — your ovaries keep working. But not having periods anymore to mark the transition raises a genuinely tricky question: how will you know when menopause happens, and does the surgery itself change the timing?

## What definitely changes, and what doesn't

Removing the uterus stops menstrual bleeding immediately. It does **not** stop ovarian hormone production — your ovaries continue releasing estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone on their own timeline, so you won't experience hot flashes, night sweats, or other menopause symptoms as a direct result of the surgery itself. This is the key difference from [surgical menopause](/groups/surgical-menopause/), which specifically means both ovaries were also removed, causing an immediate hormone drop.

## Does the surgery itself move up the timing of menopause?

This is where the research genuinely disagrees, and we think it's more honest to say so than to pick a side.

- A study following **94 women** after total abdominal hysterectomy (with ovaries conserved or one ovary removed) found their **average age at menopause was significantly lower** than a comparison group, and that **younger age at surgery was linked to earlier menopause** [@ahn-hysterectomy-ovarian-function-2002].
- A separate study comparing **29 women** who had a hysterectomy with both ovaries preserved (at age 40 or younger) against **42 menopausal controls** found **no statistically significant difference** in average age at menopause (49.7 vs. 50.1 years) [@atay-hysterectomy-ovarian-preservation-2007].

Both are small studies, and neither is a large, modern, well-powered trial that would let us confidently settle this question. The plausible biological mechanism some researchers propose — that the surgery may disrupt blood supply to the ovaries — is reasonable, but the actual evidence for whether it meaningfully changes *your* personal timeline is genuinely mixed. We're grading this **Limited** because of that disagreement, not because the question hasn't been studied at all.

## How you'll actually know menopause has happened

Without periods to track, you'll need to rely on **symptoms** rather than a calendar. Watch for the classic menopause symptoms — [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/), [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/), new sleep disruption, vaginal dryness — emerging at some point, typically around the same general age range as the rest of the population, though possibly somewhat earlier based on the research above. A blood test (FSH level) can sometimes help confirm where you are if symptoms are ambiguous, though it isn't a precise calendar either. If hot flashes or other symptoms do appear, our [does it work? evidence directory](/does-it-work/) covers the treatment options with real evidence behind them.

## What to track

Because you can't use "12 months without a period" to confirm menopause the way most women do, logging new symptoms as they appear in our [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) is the most practical way to build a picture over time, and gives your doctor something concrete if you want a blood test to help clarify where you are.
