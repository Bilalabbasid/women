Menopause is usually diagnosed from your history, not a test: if you're 45 or over and have gone 12 months in a row without a period while not using hormonal birth control, that confirms it [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @straw-10-2012]. Blood tests for follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) are mainly used under 45, when menopause may be coming early [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## How is menopause diagnosed?

For most people, menopause is diagnosed by looking back at your periods: once you've gone 12 months in a row with no period or spotting, you've reached it [@nia-what-is-menopause; @straw-10-2012]. A doctor will usually ask about your age, your symptoms and your family history to judge whether the menopause transition is the likely cause [@nia-what-is-menopause].

UK guidance (NICE NG23) says that in otherwise healthy people aged 45 and over, clinicians should identify menopause this way, without lab tests, as long as you're not using hormonal contraception [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. US sources describe the same 12-month rule [@nia-what-is-menopause]. You can count the months privately with our [12-month period-free counter](/tools/12-month-counter/).

How it works depends on your age:

| Your age | How menopause is usually identified | Are blood tests used? |
|---|---|---|
| 45 or over | 12 months without a period, or symptoms if you've had a hysterectomy [@nice-ng23-recommendations] | Not routinely [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @medlineplus-fsh-test-2023] |
| 40 to 45 | Symptoms, including changes in your cycle [@nice-ng23-recommendations] | An FSH test may be considered [@nice-ng23-recommendations] |
| Under 40 | Symptoms, including no periods or infrequent periods [@nice-ng23-recommendations] | Yes: raised FSH on 2 tests, 4 to 6 weeks apart [@nice-ng23-recommendations] |

## Is there a blood test for menopause?

Yes, an FSH blood test exists, but most people over 45 don't need one, because a high FSH level is a normal part of perimenopause and menopause [@medlineplus-fsh-test-2023]. At 45 and over, NICE advises against using other hormone tests too, including anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), inhibin, estradiol, or ovary scans [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

FSH testing is more useful when menopause may be coming early. NICE says to consider it only between 40 and 45 for people with symptoms and cycle changes, and under 40 when menopause is suspected [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. MedlinePlus lists the same situations, plus symptoms that might be menopause when the cause isn't clear [@medlineplus-fsh-test-2023].

For what the numbers can and can't tell you during the run-up to menopause, see [is there a blood test for perimenopause?](/perimenopause/blood-tests/).

## Do home menopause tests work?

Home menopause tests can tell you whether FSH in your urine is raised, but they can't tell you whether you've reached menopause [@fda-home-menopause-test]. The FDA says they detect FSH accurately about 9 out of 10 times, yet doctors wouldn't use one on its own [@fda-home-menopause-test].

- **Results swing**: FSH can rise and fall during your cycle, and you can still get pregnant while that's happening [@fda-home-menopause-test].
- **Many things affect the result**: whether you used first-morning urine, drank a lot of water, or use (or recently stopped) the pill, a birth control patch, hormone therapy or estrogen supplements [@fda-home-menopause-test].
- **A negative result doesn't rule it out**: if you have symptoms, you may still be in perimenopause or menopause [@fda-home-menopause-test].
- **Don't use it for birth control decisions**: the FDA warns not to stop contraception based on a home test result [@fda-home-menopause-test].

## Can menopause be diagnosed while on birth control?

Not with the usual methods. The 12-month period count only applies when you're not using hormonal contraception, and NICE notes that menopause is hard to identify in people taking hormonal treatments [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. NICE also advises against using an FSH test in people on combined (estrogen plus progestogen) contraception or high-dose progestogen [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

Because menopause can't be confirmed until a full year after your last period, the National Institute on Aging advises using birth control for at least 12 months after your last period if you don't want to get pregnant [@nia-what-is-menopause]. Our guide to [contraception in perimenopause](/perimenopause/contraception/) covers when it's usually safe to stop.

## How is menopause diagnosed after a hysterectomy?

If you've had your uterus removed but kept your ovaries, you have no periods to count, so the diagnosis is based on your symptoms. At 45 and over, NICE says clinicians should go by the type and combination of symptoms you have, such as hot flashes (hot flushes in the UK) and night sweats [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. See [hysterectomy with ovaries kept](/groups/hysterectomy-ovaries-retained/) for more.

## How is early or premature menopause diagnosed?

Under 45, blood tests play a bigger part, and they are most useful before 40 [@owh-early-premature-menopause-2026]. For premature ovarian insufficiency (POI, menopause before 40), NICE sets out these steps [@nice-ng23-recommendations]:

- **History first**: your doctor takes into account past medical or surgical treatment and family history.
- **Two blood tests**: POI is diagnosed from menopause symptoms, including no periods or infrequent periods, plus raised FSH on 2 blood samples taken 4 to 6 weeks apart.
- **Never one test**: POI should not be diagnosed from a single blood test, and AMH isn't used routinely.
- **Specialist help**: if the diagnosis is unclear, you should be referred to a menopause or reproductive medicine specialist.

Read more about [premature ovarian insufficiency](/groups/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/), or see the [average age of menopause](/menopause/age/) for what counts as early.

## Could it be something else?

Yes. Some other conditions cause symptoms that overlap with menopause, which is one reason a doctor asks about your history. An underactive thyroid can cause irregular or heavier periods, tiredness and low mood [@medlineplus-hypothyroidism], and an overactive thyroid can cause irregular periods, sweating, feeling hot and sleep problems [@medlineplus-hyperthyroidism]. High FSH can also be linked to conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and thyroid or adrenal disorders [@medlineplus-fsh-test-2023].

## Questions to ask your clinician

:::questions
- Given my age and the date of my last period, can we confirm menopause from my history alone?
- Since I use a hormonal IUD (or hormonal contraception), how should we assess whether I have passed menopause and when is it safe to stop contraception?
- Could anything else, such as a thyroid problem, be causing my symptoms?
- What symptoms or preventive health checks (such as bone density) should we review now that my periods have stopped?
:::
