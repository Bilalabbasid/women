Heavier, longer periods are common in [perimenopause](/perimenopause/), because irregular ovulation can let the lining of the uterus build up too thick [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. You don't have to put up with it: treatments range from tablets taken during your period to a hormonal IUD [@nhs-heavy-periods; @acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. Get checked if bleeding lasts more than 7 days, soaks a pad every hour, or comes between periods [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq].

## Why are my periods so heavy in perimenopause?

In perimenopause, your ovaries release an egg in some months and not in others [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. When ovulation is irregular, the lining of the uterus can become too thick, and shedding a thicker lining can mean a heavier period [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. ACOG notes this is common in perimenopause [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq].

Cycles also become less predictable, with cycle lengths that keep changing by 7 days or more [@straw-10-2012]. In US studies of women going through menopause, both short and long cycles were more likely to be cycles where no egg was released [@harlow-menstruation-menopause-transition-2011]. So a heavy period can follow a long gap, or come sooner than you expected. See [irregular periods in perimenopause](/perimenopause/irregular-periods/) for more on changing cycle patterns.

## What counts as a heavy period?

US and UK health bodies use similar signs [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @cdc-heavy-menstrual-bleeding; @nhs-heavy-periods]:

- **Fast flow**: soaking through one or more pads or tampons every hour for several hours in a row, or changing them after less than 2 hours.
- **Doubling up**: needing a pad and a tampon together, or more than one pad at a time.
- **Night changes**: getting up in the night to change, or bleeding through to clothes or bedding.
- **Long periods**: bleeding for more than 7 days.
- **Large clots**: clots the size of a quarter or larger.
- **Effects on your life**: skipping activities or work, or feeling tired or short of breath a lot.

## Is flooding normal in perimenopause?

Sudden heavy gushes, often called flooding, aren't a normal part of perimenopause to wait out. Heavier flow can happen as cycles change, yet ACOG lists heavy bleeding during a period, and bleeding heavier or longer than your usual, as not normal [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. If you're bleeding through to clothes or bedding, that meets the signs of a heavy period above, so it's worth getting checked [@nhs-heavy-periods].

## When to see a doctor about heavy periods

See a doctor if heavy periods affect your daily life or have gone on for some time, if you have severe period pain, if you bleed between periods or after sex, or if heavy periods come with pain when peeing, having a bowel movement or having sex [@nhs-heavy-periods]. US guidance adds bleeding that lasts longer than 7 days and periods that usually come less than 21 days apart [@mayo-perimenopause-symptoms-causes-2026]. ACOG suggests talking with an ob-gyn about any bleeding changes [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. Our guide on [when to see a doctor about perimenopause](/perimenopause/when-to-see-a-doctor/) covers how to prepare.

:::urgent
Contact a clinician without waiting for a routine appointment if you soak through one or more pads or tampons every hour for several hours in a row [@cdc-heavy-menstrual-bleeding], or if bleeding makes you feel weak or lightheaded [@medlineplus-vaginal-uterine-bleeding]. Any bleeding after 12 months with no period also needs checking, even a small amount [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding].
:::

## Can heavy periods cause anemia?

Yes. Losing a lot of blood each month can lead to iron-deficiency anemia, and severe anemia can cause shortness of breath and raise the risk of heart problems [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. Early signs include feeling weak or more tired than usual, especially with exercise, and trouble concentrating [@medlineplus-iron-deficiency-anemia]. A doctor may order blood tests to check for it [@nhs-heavy-periods].

## What else can cause heavy bleeding?

Irregular ovulation is a common cause in perimenopause, but not the only one [@medlineplus-vaginal-uterine-bleeding]. A doctor can check for other causes, such as:

- Fibroids, polyps or adenomyosis (lining tissue growing into the wall of the uterus) [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]
- Endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease [@nhs-heavy-periods]
- An underactive thyroid or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]
- A bleeding disorder, or medicines such as blood thinners and aspirin [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]
- A copper IUD, especially in its first year [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]
- Pregnancy-related problems, such as a miscarriage [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]
- Less often, cancer of the uterine lining, which is one reason heavy bleeding should be checked [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @nhs-heavy-periods]

## What tests might I have?

Your doctor will ask about your health, medicines, birth control and periods, and may do a pelvic exam, a pregnancy test and blood tests [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @nhs-heavy-periods]. Depending on your symptoms and age, you may be offered [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]:

- **Ultrasound**: sound waves make a picture of the uterus and ovaries.
- **Endometrial biopsy**: a small sample of the uterine lining is checked under a microscope.
- **Hysteroscopy**: a thin, lighted scope looks inside the uterus.
- **Sonohysterography or MRI**: more detailed views of the uterus.

## What treatments help heavy periods in perimenopause?

Heavy periods don't always need treatment, but there are options if they're affecting your life [@nhs-heavy-periods]. Medicines are usually tried first [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]:

- **Hormonal IUD (intrauterine system) or the combined pill**: hormonal birth control can lighten flow, make periods more regular, or stop bleeding altogether [@nhs-heavy-periods; @acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq].
- **Tranexamic acid**: a prescription tablet that reduces bleeding, taken each month starting at the beginning of your period [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @nhs-heavy-periods].
- **Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs)**: medicines such as ibuprofen, naproxen or mefenamic acid can reduce bleeding and ease cramps [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq; @nhs-heavy-periods].
- **Hormone therapy**: ACOG says it can help heavy bleeding in perimenopause, with its benefits and risks weighed first [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq].

If medicines don't help, or a condition such as fibroids is the cause, a specialist may offer a procedure [@nhs-heavy-periods]. Options include endometrial ablation (removing or destroying the uterine lining), removing fibroids, or a hysterectomy [@nhs-heavy-periods; @acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. ## Do I still need birth control?

Yes, if you don't want to get pregnant. Pregnancy is still possible while you're having periods, so Mayo Clinic advises birth control until you've gone 12 months without one [@mayo-perimenopause-symptoms-causes-2026]. After endometrial ablation, pregnancy is unlikely but possible and risky, so birth control is still needed until menopause [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. See [birth control in perimenopause](/perimenopause/contraception/).

## Track it before your appointment

ACOG suggests tracking several cycles before your visit: the date each period starts, how many days you bleed, and how heavy the flow is [@acog-heavy-menstrual-bleeding-faq]. Note how often you change pads or tampons, any clots, and any bleeding between periods.
