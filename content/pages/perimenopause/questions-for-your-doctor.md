Most medical appointments last between 10 and 15 minutes. In that short window, trying to describe months of erratic sleep, mood swings, cycle changes, and temperature spikes without a structured plan often leads to rushed conversations and generic advice [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Understanding where you stand in the broader [perimenopause transition](/perimenopause/) helps focus this conversation.

Walking into your appointment with a concise list of your top three symptoms, a two-week tracking record, and clear questions ensures your time is focused on practical medical decisions. It also helps to bring details on any over-the-counter options you have tried, such as [magnesium for sleep](/does-it-work/magnesium/).

## How to structure your 10-minute visit

Doctors appreciate clear, concise organization. Frame your conversation in three quick stages:

1. **State your main problem first (1 minute)**: "My sleep has been broken by 3 a.m. night sweats four nights a week for three months, and it is significantly affecting my daytime work performance."
2. **Present your objective log (2 minutes)**: Hand your clinician a one-page summary from the [Symptom tracker and doctor report](/tools/symptom-tracker/) or the completed [Perimenopause symptoms checklist](/tools/symptoms-checklist/).
3. **Ask targeted decision questions (5 to 7 minutes)**: Use the structured questions below to evaluate treatment and diagnostic options.

## Core appointment questions

:::questions
- Based on my age, irregular cycle timing, and symptom history, does this fit the clinical diagnosis of perimenopause?
- Are there non-hormonal conditions we should rule out with standard baseline blood tests, such as complete blood count, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), ferritin, or vitamin D?
- Am I a suitable candidate for systemic menopausal hormone therapy, and how do transdermal patches compare to oral options for my health profile?
- If hormone therapy is not advised or if I prefer non-hormonal treatment, what prescription medications or cognitive behavioral strategies have proven clinical evidence?
- If we start a new treatment today, what specific changes should I monitor, and when will we schedule a follow-up review?
:::

## What to do if your doctor says you are "too young"

A common obstacle women face in their early 40s is hearing that their hormone blood tests are normal or that they are too young for perimenopause. 

Guidelines from NICE and The Menopause Society state clearly that hormone blood tests are unreliable in women aged 45 and older because estradiol and FSH levels surge and drop constantly [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Furthermore, perimenopause routinely begins between ages 40 and 45.

If your provider dismisses your symptoms:
- Emphasize the functional impact: "Even if my blood levels happened to be normal this morning, these nighttime awakenings and cycle shifts are disrupting my daily functioning."
- Ask to document your discussion: "Could we note in my chart that we discussed these perimenopause symptoms and decided to defer treatment for now?"
- Request a referral to a certified menopause specialist accredited by The Menopause Society or British Menopause Society.

## Documentation checklist to bring

- **Your printed symptom summary**: generated directly from our on-device tools without sending data over the internet.
- **Your complete medication and supplement list**: including daily dosages of any over-the-counter products.
- **Key dates**: the first day of your last three menstrual periods.
