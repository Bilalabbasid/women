Clinicians and researchers use a framework called **STRAW+10** (Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop) to describe where someone is in the menopause transition, based on changes in menstrual bleeding [@straw-10-2012]. It's a useful map for understanding why your experience might be different from a friend's, even at a similar age.

## The two stages of perimenopause

| Stage | What defines it | Typical duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Early menopausal transition** | A persistent difference of **7 days or more** in the length of consecutive cycles, recurring within 10 cycles [@straw-10-2012] | Variable; can last several years |
| **Late menopausal transition** | At least one interval of **60 days or more** without a period [@straw-10-2012] | **1 to 3 years on average** [@straw-10-2012] |
| **Menopause** | Confirmed 12 months after the final period | A single point in time, not a stage |

In practice, [irregular periods](/perimenopause/irregular-periods/) — cycles that are shorter, longer, or less predictable than before — are usually the first sign that early transition has begun. See [how long does perimenopause last](/perimenopause/how-long/) for how these stages add up over time, and [perimenopause vs. menopause](/perimenopause/vs-menopause/) for how the whole timeline fits together.

## When the staging framework doesn't apply

STRAW+10 is built entirely on menstrual bleeding patterns, so it doesn't work for everyone [@straw-10-2012]:

- **After a hysterectomy**, there's no bleeding pattern left to track, so staging relies on symptoms (and sometimes hormone testing) instead.
- **Using hormonal contraception** (the pill, hormonal IUD, implant) changes or masks your natural bleeding pattern, making cycle-based staging unreliable.
- **Under age 40**, a different diagnostic pathway applies — see our [premature ovarian insufficiency](/groups/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/) guide.
