Perimenopause isn't one uniform experience that simply "happens" and then ends — different things change on different timelines, and some patterns genuinely surprise people. Here's an honest breakdown using long-term tracking data, rather than a generic "symptoms worsen then improve" story.

For how the stages are actually defined by your cycle pattern, see our [stages of perimenopause](/perimenopause/stages/) guide — this page focuses on what changes over calendar time instead.

## Body composition: a steadier change than the scale suggests

Here's a genuinely counterintuitive finding from the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), which tracked body composition using DEXA scans over the menopause transition in thousands of women: **overall weight didn't actually accelerate at the start of the transition** [@greendale-swan-body-composition-2019]. Weight climbed at a steady, linear rate through premenopause, and that trajectory simply **flattened out** after the transition ended — no sudden jump at perimenopause itself.

What *did* change substantially: **the rate of fat gain doubled**, and **lean (muscle) mass began declining**, right at the start of the transition — and both of these continued until about **2 years after the final period**, before leveling off [@greendale-swan-body-composition-2019]. In plain terms: the number on the scale may not tell the real story. Your body composition — more fat, less muscle — can shift meaningfully during this window even if your weight looks stable, which is part of why [strength training](/does-it-work/exercise/) is emphasized so heavily for this life stage specifically, rather than weight management alone.

## Mood: don't assume it resolves once periods stop

This is the pattern we think gets understated most: a SWAN analysis following **1,551 women for a median of 19 years** found the odds of **high depressive symptoms were actually greater in postmenopause than in premenopause** (OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.09–2.04) — though this difference wasn't statistically significant during perimenopause itself compared with premenopause [@kravitz-swan-postmenopause-depression-2022].

Even more useful: the single strongest predictor of postmenopausal mood wasn't age or hormone levels — it was a woman's **own depressive symptom pattern in the years leading up to her final period**, independent of other health and psychosocial factors [@kravitz-swan-postmenopause-depression-2022]. If mood has been a real, ongoing issue through your transition, this data is a good reason not to simply wait for menopause to "fix" it — see our [low mood and depression](/symptoms/depression/) guide for evidence-based next steps, and raise it with your doctor directly rather than assuming it resolves on its own.

## What this means for tracking your own experience

These two findings point to the same broader lesson: **some things that feel stable on the surface (weight) can be changing underneath (body composition), and some things you might expect to resolve (mood) may not resolve automatically.** This is exactly why tracking specific measures — not just how you feel in general — matters.

Logging your symptoms, weight, and mood over time in our [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) can help you and your clinician see real patterns, rather than relying on memory or assumptions about what "should" be happening at your stage.
