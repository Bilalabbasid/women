Estrogen plays a central role in regulating bone turnover. After [menopause](/menopause/) and throughout [postmenopause](/postmenopause/), permanently lower estrogen levels accelerate bone resorption relative to bone formation, increasing the risk of osteopenia, osteoporosis, and fractures [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Bone protection is even more urgent if menopause occurred early or before age 40 ([premature ovarian insufficiency](/groups/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/)) [@nice-ng23].

## What has strong clinical evidence for protecting bone

### 1. High-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT)

In the landmark **LIFTMOR randomized controlled trial**, postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis completed 8 months of twice-weekly, 30-minute **supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT)** [@liftmor-trial-bone-density]. Compared with a home-based low-intensity exercise control group, the HiRIT group achieved significant improvements in **lumbar spine and femoral neck bone mineral density**, muscle strength, and functional performance [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

Read our full breakdown in [Does exercise help menopause symptoms?](/does-it-work/exercise/). If you already have diagnosed osteoporosis or vertebral fractures, always work with a qualified clinician or physical therapist for supervised progressive loading [@liftmor-trial-bone-density].

### 2. Menopausal hormone therapy (HRT)

The Menopause Society's 2022 Position Statement confirms that systemic [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) prevents bone loss and reduces fracture risk in postmenopausal women, with the most favorable overall benefit-risk ratio in women under age 60 or within 10 years of menopause onset who have no contraindications [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
