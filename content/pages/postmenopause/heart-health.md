Cardiovascular risk rises for everyone with age, but the [menopause](/menopause/) transition marks a notable inflection point. A 2026 narrative review describes the transition as accompanied by **substantially increased cardiovascular disease risk**, which intensifies after age 50 — the proportion of women at intermediate-to-high cardiovascular risk nearly **doubles between ages 51 and 55** [@khalifey-hrt-cardiovascular-2026]. The same review notes that [hot flashes and night sweats](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) themselves correlate with increased cardiovascular risk, not just with discomfort.

## Why HRT isn't used to prevent heart disease

This surprises a lot of people: NICE guideline NG23 recommends **not** offering combined or oestrogen-only HRT for primary or secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Preventing heart disease and treating menopause symptoms are two different clinical questions, even though both involve the same medication.

At the same time, NICE notes that **coronary heart disease risk does not increase** with combined or estrogen-only HRT in women with no personal history of it [@nice-ng23-recommendations] — so taking HRT for symptom relief isn't understood to raise CHD risk on its own, even though it isn't prescribed to lower it either.

## The "timing hypothesis"

Research on HRT and cardiovascular outcomes has converged on what's called the **timing hypothesis**: starting HRT within 10 years of menopause onset, or before age 60, may be associated with a more favorable cardiovascular profile, while starting later may carry more risk [@khalifey-hrt-cardiovascular-2026]. This is the same "timing window" referenced throughout our [HRT guidance](/treatments/hormone-therapy/is-it-right-for-me/) — it isn't a separate rule, just the same principle applied specifically to cardiovascular outcomes.

Outcomes also vary by **formulation, route, and individual risk factors** [@khalifey-hrt-cardiovascular-2026]. Route matters concretely for stroke and clotting: stroke risk is unlikely to rise with transdermal estrogen but does rise with oral estrogen, and the same pattern holds for blood clots [@nice-ng23-recommendations] — see [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/) for the full picture.

Even with this research, the 2026 review is clear that **significant uncertainties persist**, due to how varied the existing trials are and how underrepresented some populations remain in the research [@khalifey-hrt-cardiovascular-2026].

## What actually helps protect your heart

The modern approach favors individualized care: knowing your personal risk factors (blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, family history, smoking), choosing HRT formulation and route with cardiovascular safety in mind if you use it, and standard cardiovascular prevention — physical activity, discussed in our [exercise evidence guide](/does-it-work/exercise/), being one well-supported piece of that.
