Yes, anxiety can be part of perimenopause and menopause [@nice-ng23-recommendations], and it is closely tied to hot flashes and poor sleep. Night sweats can wake you, and broken sleep can leave you irritable or low [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause]. For ongoing anxiety, talking therapy (CBT) is the best-studied treatment [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].

## How are hot flashes, insomnia and anxiety connected?

They can feed each other: hot flashes at night often wake you up, lack of sleep can make you irritable or low, and stress and anxiety go hand in hand with menopause-related insomnia [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause; @baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018]. UK guidance says menopause mood changes can range from minor to severe and last a short or long time [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

- **Hot flashes and sleep**: In a sleep-lab study of 34 women in perimenopause, about 7 in 10 hot flashes came with waking up [@de-zambotti-hot-flashes-sleep-2014]. In the large US SWAN study, women with moderate to severe hot flashes were almost three times as likely to report waking often at night [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018].
- **Sleep and mood**: Lack of sleep can make you irritable or depressed. Some research suggests waking up may itself set off a hot flash, not only the other way around [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
- **Anxiety and sleep**: Stress, going over worries again and again, and anxiety overlap with menopause-related insomnia [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018]. Trouble falling or staying asleep is also a common sign of anxiety [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].
- **Hot flashes and anxiety**: A 2026 review of 12 studies found women with more hot flashes tended to report more anxiety, but the link was weak and the reviewers were not confident in it [@neda-stepan-vms-mood-systematic-review-2026]. These studies show the two often happen together; they can't prove one causes the other.
- **Why it's worth raising early**: In SWAN, high anxiety before the final period was one of several factors linked to more depressive symptoms after menopause. Poor sleep and frequent hot flashes were others [@kravitz-swan-postmenopause-depression-2022].

## What helps hot flashes, insomnia and anxiety?

Each one has its own best-studied treatment, and treating one can ease the others. More detail is on our pages for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and [menopause insomnia](/symptoms/insomnia/).

| Symptom | Things to try first | Treatments with the best evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hot flashes and night sweats** | A cooler bedroom, a fan, layered bedding and clothes; less alcohol, spicy food and caffeine [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do] | Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]; CBT and SSRI/SNRI medicines are among the recommended non-hormonal options [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |
| **Insomnia** | A regular sleep schedule, no late-day caffeine, little or no alcohol, a quiet bedroom at a comfortable temperature [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause] | CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause; @baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018] |
| **Anxiety** | Less caffeine, enough sleep, exercise or mindfulness [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder] | CBT and SSRI/SNRI medicines [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder] |

Cooling down and avoiding triggers can make hot flashes easier to live with, but The Menopause Society did not find them effective enough to recommend as a treatment [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## What anxiety can feel like

The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) lists these common signs of ongoing anxiety [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder]:

- Worrying a lot about everyday things, and finding it hard to stop
- Feeling irritable, restless, or "on edge"
- Trouble concentrating
- Trouble falling or staying asleep, or feeling tired
- Headaches, muscle aches, stomachaches, or tension

If anxiety comes in sudden, intense waves, see our page on [panic attacks in perimenopause](/symptoms/panic-attacks/).

## Other causes to rule out

Not every new wave of anxiety in midlife is menopause. A doctor can examine you and check for other causes [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder], such as:

- An overactive thyroid, which can cause nervousness, anxiety, and irritability, along with a fast or uneven heartbeat and weight loss despite a bigger appetite [@nhs-overactive-thyroid-symptoms]
- Heart rhythm changes, which can feel like anxiety but have their own causes (see [heart palpitations](/symptoms/heart-palpitations/))

## Things you can try tonight

:::try
1. Cut back on caffeine, especially later in the day [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder; @nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
2. Keep your bedroom cool, with a fan and bedding in layers you can throw off [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].
3. Go to bed and get up at the same times each day [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
4. Try a stress-easing habit such as exercise, mindfulness, or meditation [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].
5. Write down when anxiety, hot flashes, and wake-ups happen, so you can show your doctor the pattern.
:::

NIMH notes these habits can ease anxiety but can't replace treatment when you need it [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].

## What can help

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**: NIMH calls CBT the well-studied "gold standard" talking therapy for ongoing anxiety [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder]. The Menopause Society also recommends CBT for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] :grade{id="cbt-hypnosis-vms"}.
- **CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)**: A version of CBT made for sleep problems has been shown to improve sleep in women with menopause symptoms [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause; @baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018] :grade{id="cbt-sleep-vms"}.
- **SSRI and SNRI antidepressants**: NIMH says these can help treat anxiety, usually take several weeks to work, and may cause side effects such as headaches, nausea, or trouble sleeping [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder]. They are also recommended for hot flashes [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] :grade{id="ssri-snri-vms"}. See [SSRIs and SNRIs](/treatments/nonhormonal/ssris-snris/).
- **Other anxiety medicines**: NIMH notes benzodiazepines can calm anxiety quickly, but the body can get used to them and people can come to depend on them, so they are often prescribed only for short periods. Buspirone takes 3 to 4 weeks to work fully [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].
- **Treating night sweats and broken sleep**: Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] :grade{id="mht-vms"}, and several [non-hormonal options](/treatments/nonhormonal/) work too. In research, frequent hot flashes and poor sleep go hand in hand with low mood [@kravitz-swan-postmenopause-depression-2022; @neda-stepan-vms-mood-systematic-review-2026] :grade{id="treat-vms-for-mood"}.
- **Herbal "calming" blends and CBD**: The Menopause Society does not recommend over-the-counter herbal remedies or cannabinoids for menopause symptoms [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] :grade{id="otc-mood-supplements"}. See our [Does It Work? evidence directory](/does-it-work/).

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment if [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder; @nhs-overactive-thyroid-symptoms]:

- Anxiety is new, getting worse, or getting in the way of work, sleep, or relationships
- You also notice a fast or irregular heartbeat, weight loss, or other signs of an overactive thyroid
- You've started treatment and it isn't helping (finding the right one can take some trial and error)

:::urgent
**If you are thinking about harming yourself or feel unable to stay safe, get help now.**

- **US:** Call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), free and confidential, 24/7 [@lifeline-988]. In a life-threatening situation, call **911** [@nimh-generalized-anxiety-disorder].
- **US:** Call **911** or go to the emergency room if anxiety comes with palpitations that don't go away, chest pain, shortness of breath, or fainting [@nhs-heart-palpitations].

**If you're in the UK:** call **999** or go to A&E if a life is at risk [@nhs-urgent-help-mental-health] or for palpitations with chest pain, shortness of breath, or fainting [@nhs-heart-palpitations]. Call **NHS 111** and choose the mental health option for urgent advice [@nhs-urgent-help-mental-health]. Samaritans are on **116 123**, free, any time [@samaritans-contact].
:::
