Cramps in perimenopause are usually period pain, and they can carry on for as long as you still have periods [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq; @nhs-period-pain-2026]. The cramp is your uterus (womb) contracting: chemicals called prostaglandins, made in its lining, make its muscle tighten [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq]. Painful periods are common in midlife: in a British study, 44.1% of women aged 46 reported them [@abdullayev-bcs70-menstrual-symptoms-2026]. Cramps that are new or getting worse can have other causes, and cramps with any bleeding after menopause should always be checked [@nhs-period-pain-2026; @nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding].

**Perimenopause cramps at a glance**

- **What a cramp is**: prostaglandins make the muscles and blood vessels of the uterus contract. Levels peak on the first day of a period, which is why pain tends to ease after a few days [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].
- **How common**: 44.1% of women aged 46 in the 1970 British Cohort Study reported painful periods [@abdullayev-bcs70-menstrual-symptoms-2026].
- **Worse cramps aren't just "the change"**: the NHS says to see a GP if your periods become more painful, heavier or irregular [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
- **What helps**: heat on your tummy, gentle exercise, and painkillers such as ibuprofen or paracetamol [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
- **After menopause**: any bleeding once you've gone 12 months without a period needs checking, even a small amount or a single episode [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding].

:::urgent
**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you have pelvic pain and it's severe, getting worse, or hurts when you move or touch the area; you feel faint, dizzy or lightheaded, or pass out; you have pain in the tip of your shoulder; you have heavy bleeding from your vagina; or you have difficulty breathing or suddenly feel confused [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025].

**Get medical advice the same day** if you have pelvic pain and you are or may be pregnant, have a very high temperature or feel hot, cold or shivery, or have unusual bleeding, discharge or fluid from your vagina [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025]. Sudden or severe pain when your period is more than a week late and you have been sexually active needs a doctor right away [@medlineplus-painful-menstrual-periods].

If you're in the UK: call 999 or go to A&E for the emergency signs above, and call NHS 111 for the same-day signs [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025].
:::

## Are cramps a sign of perimenopause?

Not on their own. Cramps come with periods, and you keep having periods all through perimenopause, so period cramps can continue until your periods stop [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. What marks perimenopause is a change in the cycle itself: periods that come closer together or further apart, get heavier or lighter, or are skipped [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. A cycle that is consistently 7 days or more different in length suggests early perimenopause [@mayo-perimenopause-symptoms-causes-2026]. See our guide to [irregular periods in perimenopause](/perimenopause/irregular-periods/).

Research on cramps in perimenopause specifically is thin. The best long-term data we found comes from the Seattle Midlife Women's Health Study, which tracked abdominal pain (not only period cramps) through the menopause transition. Pain tended to be milder as women got older and when levels of an estrogen measured in their urine were higher, and worse when they felt anxious. The stage of the transition itself was not linked with how bad the pain was [@callan-seattle-abdominal-pain-2019].

## Why can perimenopause cramps feel like contractions?

Because they are contractions. Prostaglandins, made in the lining of the uterus, cause its muscles and blood vessels to contract. Their level is high on the first day of a period and falls as the lining is shed, which is why the pain usually eases after the first few days [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq]. The NHS describes period pain as cramps in the tummy that can spread to the back and thighs, usually lasting up to 3 days [@nhs-period-pain-2026]. Severe cramps can come with diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, headache and dizziness [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].

One cause of severe period pain at this age is **adenomyosis**, where the lining of the uterus grows into its muscle wall. Its symptoms usually affect women over 35 and include painful periods, sometimes severe enough to stop your usual activities, and heavy periods [@nhs-adenomyosis-2026]. It is more common in older women who have had children [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].

## Why are my cramps worse in perimenopause?

We found no evidence that perimenopause itself makes period cramps worse, so cramps that are getting worse deserve a check. ACOG says ordinary period pain often becomes less painful as women get older, while pain caused by a condition in the reproductive organs tends to get worse over time, often lasts longer than normal cramps, and may start a few days before a period or carry on after it ends [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].

Conditions that can cause painful periods later in life include [@nhs-period-pain-2026; @medlineplus-painful-menstrual-periods; @acog-dysmenorrhea-faq]:

- **Fibroids**: non-cancerous growths in or around the uterus, which mainly affect women who haven't been through menopause. They can cause heavy or painful periods, stomach pain and lower back pain [@nhs-fibroids-2026].
- **Adenomyosis** and **endometriosis**: tissue like the lining of the uterus growing in its muscle wall or elsewhere in the pelvis [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease**: an infection of the uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
- **A copper IUD** [@medlineplus-painful-menstrual-periods].

Pain can also go with heavy bleeding: the CDC lists constant lower stomach pain during periods among the signs of heavy menstrual bleeding [@cdc-heavy-menstrual-bleeding]. If your periods have also become heavier, see [heavy periods in perimenopause](/perimenopause/heavy-periods/).

## Perimenopause cramps but no period: what could it be?

A cramp with no bleed has several possible explanations, and the feeling alone can't tell you which:

- **A late or skipped period**: in perimenopause the ovaries release an egg in some months and not others, and periods may be skipped [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. We found no research on how often period-like cramps happen without a bleed in perimenopause.
- **Ovulation pain**: a dull pain or sharp twinges on one side of the tummy, about 14 days before a period, lasting from a few minutes to 1 or 2 days [@nhs-ovulation-pain-2026].
- **Pregnancy**: still possible while you are having periods; birth control is advised until you've gone 12 months without one [@mayo-perimenopause-symptoms-causes-2026]. An ectopic pregnancy is a rare but serious cause of pelvic pain [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025]. If you could be pregnant, take a test and see our guide to [pregnancy in perimenopause](/perimenopause/pregnancy/).
- **Bowel or bladder problems**: constipation, irritable bowel syndrome and urinary tract infections are common causes of pelvic pain [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025]. Studies report more abdominal pain and [bloating](/symptoms/bloating/) in perimenopause, though a hormonal cause isn't proven [@heitkemper-chang-ibs-hormones-2009].
- **Ovarian cysts**: usually painless, but a cyst that splits, twists or grows large can cause pelvic pain ranging from a dull, heavy feeling to a sudden, sharp pain [@nhs-ovarian-cyst].
- **Adenomyosis or endometriosis**: adenomyosis can cause pelvic pain at any time in the cycle [@nhs-adenomyosis-2026]. MedlinePlus advises seeing a provider if pain happens at times other than your period, starts more than 5 days before it, or continues after it [@medlineplus-painful-menstrual-periods].

## Cramps after menopause

Once you've gone 12 months without a period, cramps are no longer period pain, so they need a different explanation.

### Menopause cramps and bleeding

Any vaginal bleeding after menopause should be checked by a doctor, even a small amount or a single episode. It usually isn't serious, but it can be a sign of cancer [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding]. ACOG lists polyps, thinning of the uterine lining, a thickened lining (endometrial hyperplasia) and endometrial cancer among the causes [@acog-perimenopausal-bleeding-faq]. If you take HRT, bleeding usually settles within 6 months; the NHS says to speak to a GP about bleeding after your periods had stopped for a while [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. Our guide to [bleeding after menopause](/postmenopause/bleeding/) explains what the checks involve.

### Menopause cramps with no bleeding

Pelvic pain after menopause is often from the bowel or bladder, such as constipation, irritable bowel syndrome or a urinary infection. It can also come from an ovarian cyst or endometriosis, and rarely from womb cancer, ovarian cancer or pelvic inflammatory disease [@nhs-pelvic-pain-2025]. A few points matter more at this stage:

- **Ovarian cysts**: most are non-cancerous, but cancerous cysts are more common after menopause, so a cyst found then is usually monitored with scans and blood tests over a year [@nhs-ovarian-cyst].
- **Endometriosis**: it can still cause symptoms after menopause, and a European menopause society guide advises surgery as the preferred treatment when it does [@emas-endometriosis-menopause-guide-2025].
- **Ovarian cancer warning signs**: the CDC says to see a doctor if pelvic pain or pressure, bloating, feeling full quickly, or a change in bathroom habits lasts 2 weeks or longer and isn't normal for you [@cdc-ovarian-cancer-symptoms].

Cramps in your legs are a different problem; the NHS lists leg cramps among the possible side effects of estrogen in HRT [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023].

## What helps period cramps in perimenopause

We found no trials of cramp treatments in perimenopausal women specifically, so this advice comes from general guidance on period pain:

- **Heat**: a warm bath, or a heat pad or hot water bottle on your tummy [@nhs-period-pain-2026; @acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].
- **Movement**: gentle exercise such as yoga, swimming, walking or cycling [@nhs-period-pain-2026]. In the British study of women aged 46, those who did the least moderate-to-vigorous activity, and those with the least regular sleep, were more likely to report painful periods; that study can't show cause and effect [@abdullayev-bcs70-menstrual-symptoms-2026].
- **Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs)**: ibuprofen and naproxen reduce prostaglandins and work best taken at the first sign of a period or pain, usually for 1 or 2 days. They aren't suitable if you have a bleeding disorder, asthma, aspirin allergy, liver damage, stomach problems or ulcers [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq]. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is an alternative [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
- **Treatments from a doctor**: prescription anti-inflammatories, a TENS machine, or hormonal contraception such as the pill, implant or injection [@nhs-period-pain-2026]. The hormonal IUD can also ease painful periods [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].
- **Treating the cause**: if a scan finds a condition such as fibroids, treating it helps [@nhs-period-pain-2026].

ACOG says vitamin B1 or magnesium may help, but there isn't enough research to recommend them [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].

:::try
1. Use a heat pad or hot water bottle wrapped in a towel on your tummy as cramps start [@nhs-period-pain-2026].
2. If NSAIDs are safe for you, take ibuprofen or naproxen at the first sign of pain rather than waiting [@acog-dysmenorrhea-faq].
3. Log cramps alongside bleeding, timing and pain level in the [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) for 2 to 3 cycles, so your doctor can see whether the pain is changing.
:::

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment if [@nhs-period-pain-2026; @medlineplus-painful-menstrual-periods; @nhs-pelvic-pain-2025]:

- your periods become more painful, heavier or irregular
- period pain stops you doing your usual activities, or treatment hasn't helped after 3 months
- pain comes at times other than your period, or you have pain during sex or when peeing or pooing
- you bleed between periods or pass blood clots with the pain
- you have a swollen tummy, lose your appetite or lose weight without trying
- pelvic pain comes and goes or doesn't go away
- you have any bleeding after 12 months without a period [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding]

Ask for an urgent appointment (or call NHS 111 in the UK) if pelvic or period pain is severe or worse than usual and painkillers haven't helped [@nhs-period-pain-2026]. Our [questions to ask your doctor](/perimenopause/questions-for-your-doctor/) can help you prepare.
