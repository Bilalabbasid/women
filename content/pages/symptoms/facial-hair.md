Noticing a few new coarse hairs on the chin, upper lip, or jawline after menopause is common — and usually not a sign that anything is medically wrong. A 2022 dermatology review found facial hirsutism in **about 50% of postmenopausal women**, the large majority with **normal androgen (testosterone) levels** [@kamp-menopause-hair-disorders-2022].

## Why it happens

Estrogen falls sharply after menopause, but ovarian and adrenal androgen (male hormone) production declines more gradually. The result is a **shift in the estrogen-to-androgen ratio**, even without an actual rise in androgens — and androgen-sensitive hair follicles on the face respond to that shift by producing coarser, more visible hair. This is why the review specifically notes that most affected women have *normal* androgen levels: the relative balance has changed, not necessarily the absolute amount [@kamp-menopause-hair-disorders-2022].

## When it's worth testing

For most women, isolated chin or lip hair after menopause doesn't need blood work. Testing for testosterone and prolactin becomes relevant specifically when hirsutism appears **together with other signs of androgen excess** — such as severe or new-onset acne, scalp hair thinning at the crown, or irregular periods if you're still cycling [@kamp-menopause-hair-disorders-2022]. That combination can point toward a condition like polycystic ovary syndrome or, rarely, an androgen-producing tumor, and is worth raising with your doctor rather than managing with removal alone.

## Managing it

This review doesn't evaluate specific hair-removal methods, so we won't claim evidence for any one option here. In practice, women typically manage facial hair with:

- **Plucking, threading, or waxing** for occasional hairs
- **Laser hair removal or electrolysis** for more persistent growth, usually requiring multiple sessions
- **Prescription topical eflornithine**, which slows regrowth (ask your doctor whether it's appropriate for you)

If hair growth is rapid in onset, unusually extensive, or accompanied by other androgen-excess signs, that pattern — not the hair itself — is the reason to see a doctor rather than a dermatologist or aesthetician alone. It's also worth checking whether you're also noticing [scalp hair thinning](/symptoms/hair-thinning/) or [new acne](/symptoms/acne/), since all three can share the same underlying hormone shift. Tracking the timing and extent of any changes in our [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) can help your doctor judge whether testing is actually warranted.
