Frozen shoulder — stiffness and pain that gradually restrict shoulder movement, sometimes for a year or more — is widely believed among clinicians to show up more often in women around midlife. But the research on menopause specifically as a cause is much thinner than the clinical folklore suggests.

## What the evidence actually shows

A 2026 pilot study looked at the diagnosis rate of adhesive capsulitis (the clinical name for frozen shoulder) between two groups of women and found it diagnosed in **7.65%** of one group compared with **3.95%** of the other — an odds ratio of 1.99 (95% CI 0.86–4.58, p=0.10) [@reinke-frozen-shoulder-hrt-2026]. That confidence interval crosses 1, and the p-value is above the conventional 0.05 threshold, meaning this difference did not reach statistical significance. The study's own framing is as a **pilot**: a smaller study designed to see whether a larger, properly powered trial is worth running — not one that can confirm a hormonal cause or a hormone therapy benefit on its own [@reinke-frozen-shoulder-hrt-2026].

In plain terms: there's a numerical difference worth studying further, but this single pilot can't tell us whether menopause hormone changes cause frozen shoulder, or whether hormone therapy would treat or prevent it.

## What's actually known to help

Regardless of what role hormones eventually turn out to play, frozen shoulder itself has well-established treatment approaches:

- **Physical therapy** with structured range-of-motion [exercises](/does-it-work/exercise/) is the usual first-line approach
- **Pain relief** (over-the-counter or prescribed) to tolerate the stretching needed for recovery
- **Corticosteroid injections** into the joint, which can shorten the painful phase for some people
- **Hydrodilatation or surgery** for cases that don't improve with the above, usually after several months of conservative treatment

## What to ask your doctor

Because frozen shoulder can take 12 to 24 months to fully resolve even with treatment, getting an early, accurate diagnosis matters more than identifying a single cause. If you're also noticing more general [joint and muscle pain](/symptoms/joint-pain/), logging both in our [symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) can help separate a frozen-shoulder-specific pattern from broader joint changes.
