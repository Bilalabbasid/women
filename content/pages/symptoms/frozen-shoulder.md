Frozen shoulder is pain and stiffness that slowly limits how far you can move your shoulder. It is most common between ages 40 and 60 and in women, which overlaps with menopause, but research hasn't shown that menopause causes it [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder; @navarro-ledesma-frozen-shoulder-estrogen-review-2025]. Physical therapy is the main treatment [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder].

## Is frozen shoulder linked to menopause?

A link is possible but not proven. Frozen shoulder most often affects people aged 40 to 60 and is more common in women than men, according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder]. That is the same age range as perimenopause and menopause, but sharing an age range doesn't show that hormones are the cause.

Here is what is and isn't known:

- **What is known**: Frozen shoulder is more common in women and in midlife [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder]. Joint and muscle pain in general is a recognized menopause symptom [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **What researchers suspect**: A 2025 review proposes that lower estrogen may weaken the body's defenses against inflammation and scarring in the joint. The author presents this as an idea still being studied, not an established fact [@navarro-ledesma-frozen-shoulder-estrogen-review-2025].
- **What isn't known**: No study we found shows that menopause causes frozen shoulder, or how much risk it adds.

## Does HRT prevent or treat frozen shoulder?

That hasn't been shown. A 2026 pilot study looked at 1,952 women aged 40 to 60 after menopause at one US health system [@reinke-frozen-shoulder-hrt-2026]. Frozen shoulder was diagnosed in **7.65%** of women not using hormone therapy and **3.95%** of those using it.

That looks like a big gap, but the study was small on the hormone therapy side (152 women), and the difference could have been due to chance. The authors describe it as a pilot to plan a larger trial, not proof that hormone therapy protects the shoulder [@reinke-frozen-shoulder-hrt-2026]. If you're weighing [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) for other menopause symptoms, the current evidence doesn't support starting it for frozen shoulder alone.

## What else makes frozen shoulder more likely?

Several things are linked to frozen shoulder [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder; @nhs-frozen-shoulder]:

- **Diabetes**: Frozen shoulder is much more common in people with diabetes, and the stiffness tends to last longer. No one knows exactly why.
- **Thyroid problems**: Both an underactive and an overactive thyroid are linked to it.
- **Other conditions**: Parkinson's disease and heart disease are also linked.
- **Not moving the arm**: It can follow a period when the arm was kept still after surgery, a broken bone, or another injury.

## What are the symptoms and stages?

The main symptoms are shoulder pain, which can be worse at night and disturb sleep, and stiffness that makes the shoulder hard to move [@nhs-frozen-shoulder]. The pain is usually a dull ache over the outer shoulder, sometimes reaching the upper arm [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder].

It usually moves through three stages [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder]:

- **Freezing**: Pain slowly gets worse and the shoulder loses movement. This lasts about 6 weeks to 9 months.
- **Frozen**: Pain may ease, but stiffness stays and daily tasks can be hard. This lasts 4 to 6 months.
- **Thawing**: Movement slowly comes back. This takes about 6 months to 2 years.

## How long does frozen shoulder last?

Frozen shoulder usually gets better on its own over time, but it can take up to 3 years [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder]. The NHS says it can take months or years, and the pain and stiffness usually go away eventually [@nhs-frozen-shoulder].

## What treatments help frozen shoulder?

Physical therapy that focuses on stretching and gentle movement is the main recommended treatment [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder]. For keeping muscles and bones strong through menopause more generally, see our [strength training evidence review](/does-it-work/exercise/). Other options include [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder; @nhs-frozen-shoulder]:

- **Pain relief**: Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen can ease pain and swelling. Stronger painkillers are usually used only for a short time because of side effects.
- **Physical therapy**: In the NHS, a course usually lasts at least 6 weeks and can include stretching, strengthening, and posture advice.
- **Steroid injections**: A doctor can inject a steroid into the shoulder joint to ease pain.
- **Hydrodilatation**: If other treatments haven't worked, a doctor injects a large amount of sterile fluid into the joint, guided by imaging, to stretch it.
- **Surgery**: If therapy doesn't help, options include moving the shoulder while you're asleep under anesthesia, or keyhole surgery to cut the tight tissue. Physical therapy afterward takes about 6 weeks to 3 months.

If you have diabetes, keeping it well controlled can help speed recovery [@aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder].

## Things you can try at home

:::try
1. Do gentle shoulder stretches and range-of-motion exercises at home [@nhs-frozen-shoulder; @aaos-orthoinfo-frozen-shoulder].
2. Skip hard workouts or gym machines you've made up yourself, which can make the pain worse [@nhs-frozen-shoulder].
3. Use a heat pack for up to 20 minutes at a time [@nhs-frozen-shoulder].
:::

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if shoulder pain and stiffness don't go away, or the pain is so bad that it's hard to move your arm and shoulder [@nhs-frozen-shoulder]. A doctor can check whether it is frozen shoulder or another cause of shoulder pain. If you also have aches in other joints, see our page on [menopause joint and muscle pain](/symptoms/joint-pain/), and note both in our [private symptom tracker](/tools/symptom-tracker/) to show your doctor.

:::urgent
Shoulder or arm pain can sometimes be a sign of a heart problem. **Call 911** if you have chest pressure, squeezing, or pain that spreads to your jaw, left arm, or between your shoulder blades, or that comes with nausea, sweating, a racing heart, or shortness of breath [@medlineplus-chest-pain].
:::
