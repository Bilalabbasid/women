As outlined in our [menopause symptoms overview](/symptoms/), bladder leaks during perimenopause and menopause happen because declining estrogen weakens the urethral lining while pelvic floor muscles lose tone and collagen support [@nams-2020-gsm]. Leaks are not an unavoidable consequence of aging: structured pelvic floor muscle training is recommended by medical guidelines as the first-line treatment and helps up to 80% of women reduce or completely resolve leaking [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence, @cochrane-pfmt-incontinence].

## Why do bladder leaks increase during menopause?

The lower urinary tract is highly sensitive to hormones [@nams-2020-gsm]. Estrogen receptors are densely concentrated throughout the bladder base (trigone), urethral mucosa, and pelvic connective tissues. 

When estrogen levels drop:
1. **The urethral seal weakens**: The mucosal lining inside the urethra thins, reducing the watertight closure pressure that keeps urine inside during physical exertion [@nams-2020-gsm].
2. **Pelvic support decreases**: Declining estrogen and age-related collagen loss weaken the muscular sling supporting the bladder and urethra.
3. **The bladder muscle becomes irritable**: Involuntary spasms of the bladder muscle (detrusor muscle) can trigger intense, sudden urges to urinate even when the bladder is only partially full.

## Understanding your leak type: stress vs urge

Treatment depends heavily on identifying which type of incontinence you are experiencing:

| Feature | Stress incontinence | Urge incontinence | Mixed incontinence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **When leaks happen** | During coughing, sneezing, laughing, running, jumping, or heavy lifting | Suddenly after a sudden, uncontrollable urge to void | A combination of both physical exertion leaks and sudden urgency |
| **Underlying cause** | Insufficient urethral closure pressure and weakened pelvic floor support | Overactive bladder muscle (detrusor instability) contracting prematurely | Both pelvic floor weakness and bladder nerve/muscle irritability |
| **Primary treatment** | Pelvic floor muscle training (Kegels) to strengthen the muscular sling [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence] | Bladder retraining, fluid timing, pelvic floor training, and medical therapy | Combined pelvic muscle rehabilitation and bladder habit adjustments |

## What can help: evidence-based steps

### 1. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)

Cochrane systematic reviews confirm that women who undertake structured pelvic floor exercises are eight times more likely to report complete resolution of leaking compared to women receiving no treatment [@cochrane-pfmt-incontinence]. 

For muscle training to work, exercises must be performed correctly: lifting upward and inward rather than pushing outward or bearing down. NICE advises a routine internal (digital) check to confirm a correct pelvic floor contraction before starting supervised training [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence]. 

App-connected biofeedback devices help ensure you contract the correct muscles and track your strength progression at home.

### 2. Bladder retraining for urge leaks

If sudden urgency is your primary challenge:
- **Avoid bladder irritants**: Caffeine, alcohol, carbonated drinks, and artificial sweeteners can irritate the bladder lining.
- **Timed voiding**: Rather than rushing to the toilet the second you feel an urge, use slow diaphragmatic breathing and pelvic floor contractions to suppress the bladder spasm, gradually extending time between visits.

### 3. Local vaginal estrogen

Because estrogen maintains urethral thickness, low-dose prescription vaginal estrogen can help reduce urgency, frequency, and recurrent urinary tract infections in postmenopausal women [@nams-2020-gsm]. Discuss with your doctor whether local estrogen is suitable for your situation.

:::urgent
Seek medical evaluation if bladder leakage is accompanied by blood in your urine (hematuria), burning or pain while peeing, high fever, inability to pass urine, or a bulging sensation at the vaginal entrance (which may indicate pelvic organ prolapse) [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence].
:::

## Questions people ask

### How long does pelvic floor training take to stop bladder leaks?
Clinical trials show significant improvement in leaking typically occurs after 8 to 12 weeks of daily pelvic floor exercises [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence]. Consistency is essential; performing exercises three times per day maintains strength.

### Should I see a pelvic health physiotherapist?
Yes. If you are unsure whether you are contracting the right muscles, if you have pelvic pain, or if exercises do not improve leaks after two months, an assessment with a pelvic health physical therapist provides invaluable tailored guidance [@nice-ng123-urinary-incontinence].
