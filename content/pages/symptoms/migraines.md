Migraine is a distinct neurological condition that disproportionately affects women, and it often changes shape during the menopause transition. A 2026 narrative review synthesizing current evidence and guideline statements from neurology, gynecology, and cardiovascular societies found that unstable estradiol and progesterone levels during [perimenopause](/perimenopause/) can worsen migraine frequency and make attacks less predictable [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026].

The same review found a clear split by migraine type: **migraine without aura often improves after menopause**, while **migraine with aura tends to persist** — and migraine with aura independently increases the risk of ischemic stroke and other vascular events, separate from any hormone therapy decision [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026].

If you're not sure whether what you have is a [headache](/symptoms/headaches/) or a true migraine, start there — the distinction changes what's safe to try.

:::urgent
Get urgent medical care for a severe headache that comes on suddenly ("thunderclap"), is the worst of your life, or comes with weakness on one side, slurred speech, confusion, or vision loss — these need immediate evaluation to rule out a stroke or other emergency, and are not typical of migraine.
:::

## Why hormone therapy decisions get more careful with migraine — especially with aura

This is one of the few places where the *route* of hormone therapy, not just the decision to use it, directly affects safety:

| Factor | What the evidence shows | Evidence grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oral estrogen** | May worsen migraine and raise vascular risk, especially with higher doses and in women who get aura [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026] | **Caution** |
| **Low-dose transdermal estrogen** (patch/gel) | Appears safer and better tolerated for migraine, and is the option The North American Menopause Society recommends for women with migraine per this review [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026] | **Preferred when HRT is used** |
| **Continuous vs. cyclic progestogen** | Continuous regimens may reduce withdrawal-related migraine attacks compared with cyclic regimens [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026] | **Moderate** |
| **Baseline stroke risk, women under 60** | Very low; unlikely to rise with transdermal combined HRT, but rises with oral estrogen-containing HRT, increasing with dose and duration [@nice-ng23-recommendations] | **Context, not a verdict for any individual** |

None of this is a substitute for an individual risk assessment — your personal and family history of stroke, blood clots, and cardiovascular disease all factor in alongside your migraine type. See our [HRT overview](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) and the [FDA boxed warning explainer](/treatments/hormone-therapy/boxed-warning/) for the broader safety picture.

## Managing migraine itself

The same review notes that standard migraine treatments — triptans, NSAIDs, beta-blockers, topiramate, and certain antidepressants — remain central during the menopause transition, with newer options (CGRP monoclonal antibodies, gepants, and ditans) offering non-vasoconstrictive alternatives for women with cardiovascular contraindications [@korn-bernstein-migraine-menopause-2026]. These are prescription decisions that need to account for your vascular, bone, and metabolic health alongside your migraine pattern.
