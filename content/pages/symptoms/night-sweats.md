Night sweats are hot flashes that happen while you sleep, and they can wake you up hot, soaked and then shivery [@nia-what-is-menopause]. Tonight, a cooler bedroom, a fan and layered bedding can make waking up drenched less miserable [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do]. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment, and The Menopause Society also recommends several non-hormone prescriptions [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

Night sweats matter because they break up sleep. Women with hot flashes and night sweats are more likely to report poor sleep during the menopause transition [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. To see your pattern, log your nights for a couple of weeks in our on-device [symptom tracker and doctor summary](/tools/symptom-tracker/).

## Is this normal, and could it be perimenopause?

Yes. Hot flashes and night sweats are the most common symptoms of menopause and affect up to 80% of women [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. UK guidance says that for otherwise healthy women aged 45 or over, recent hot flashes or sweats together with changes in your periods are usually enough to recognize perimenopause, without blood tests [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

Night sweats can start while you still have regular periods. In a large US study, frequent hot flashes and night sweats lasted about 7.4 years in total (the median), and women whose symptoms began while they still had regular periods, or early in perimenopause, had them longest [@swan-vms-duration-avis-2015].

Here is what happens. As estrogen falls, a group of brain cells next to your brain's temperature-control center becomes overactive (doctors call them KNDy neurons), and this upsets temperature control [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Even a small rise in your core temperature can then set off a hot flash [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Your body tries to cool down fast with flushing and heavy sweating, and cold shivering can follow as the sweat cools [@nia-what-is-menopause].

## Immediate steps: how to sleep cooler tonight

The National Institute on Aging suggests these simple steps.

:::try
1. Lower the temperature in your bedroom and turn on a fan [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].
2. Layer your bedding so you can push a layer off or pull it back as needed [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].
3. Try drinking small amounts of cold water before bed [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].
4. Go easy on alcohol, spicy food and caffeine, which can make menopause symptoms worse [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].
:::

Be realistic about what these steps can do. They can make nights more comfortable, but there is no strong evidence that cooling or avoiding triggers reduces how many night sweats you have [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. In one small trial of a bedtime cooling device, women said they slept better, but the number of night-time hot flashes did not change [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. If you want to compare fabrics and bedding, see our guide to [sleepwear for night sweats](/care/sleepwear-for-night-sweats/).

## What else could cause night sweats?

Menopause is a common cause, but not the only one. It's also normal to sweat at night if your room or bedding is too warm [@nhs-night-sweats]. A doctor can check for other causes, such as:

- **Anxiety**: feeling anxious is a common cause of night sweats [@nhs-night-sweats].
- **Medicines**: some antidepressants, steroids and painkillers can cause night sweats [@nhs-night-sweats], as can tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors [@medlineplus-cancer-hot-flashes-night-sweats].
- **Low blood sugar**: this can cause sweating, including at night [@nhs-night-sweats; @medlineplus-sweating].
- **Alcohol or drug use**: drinking or drug use, and withdrawal from them, can cause sweating [@nhs-night-sweats; @medlineplus-sweating].
- **An overactive thyroid**: sweating along with a fast, pounding heartbeat or weight loss can be a sign of it [@medlineplus-sweating].
- **Too much sweating in general**: a harmless condition called hyperhidrosis makes some people sweat heavily all the time [@nhs-night-sweats].
- **Infections and, less often, some cancers**: these are on the list of causes, which is why night sweats with a fever, cough or weight loss should be checked [@medlineplus-sweating; @nhs-night-sweats].

Sometimes no cause is found [@nhs-night-sweats].

## What actually helps long-term: evidence-graded treatments

Each option below shows its evidence grade. Non-hormone prescriptions usually start to work within 2 weeks [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

- **Hormone therapy (HRT)**: :grade{id="mht-vms"}. The most effective treatment. Compared with placebo, estrogen (alone or with a progestogen) cut how often hot flashes and night sweats happened each week by about 75% and made them less severe [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. It also improves sleep for women with bothersome night sweats by reducing night-time waking [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Pills and skin patches or gels work about equally well for symptoms at the same dose [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. See [hormone therapy: patch vs pill](/treatments/hormone-therapy/patch-vs-pill/).
- **NK3 receptor blockers (fezolinetant, elinzanetant)**: :grade{id="nk3-vms"}. Non-hormone pills that act directly on the brain's temperature control [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Fezolinetant (Veozah) has a boxed warning for liver injury, so liver blood tests are needed before and during treatment [@veozah-prescribing-information]. Elinzanetant (Lynkuet) can cause daytime sleepiness, needs liver tests and must not be used in pregnancy [@lynkuet-prescribing-information].
- **Low-dose SSRIs and SNRIs**: :grade{id="ssri-snri-vms"}. Several of these antidepressants, used at low doses, reduced hot flashes and night sweats in large trials [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. See [SSRIs and SNRIs](/treatments/nonhormonal/ssris-snris/).
- **Gabapentin**: :grade{id="gabapentin-vms"}. Reduces hot flashes and night sweats. Because it can cause drowsiness, taking it at bedtime may suit women whose sleep is broken by night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. See [gabapentin](/treatments/nonhormonal/gabapentin/).
- **CBT, including CBT for insomnia**: :grade{id="cbt-sleep-vms"}. In pooled trials, women who had both night sweats and insomnia improved with CBT for insomnia, and changing beliefs about sleep and night sweats was part of how CBT helped [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. See [CBT and hypnosis](/treatments/nonhormonal/cbt-and-hypnosis/).
- **Oxybutynin**: :grade{id="oxybutynin-vms"}. A bladder medicine that improved moderate to severe hot flashes in trials. Dry mouth and trouble passing urine are common, and long-term use of this type of medicine may be linked with a decline in thinking and memory, especially in older people [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
- **Cooling sleepwear, bedding and fans**: :grade{id="cooling-comfort"}. Can make nights more comfortable, but does not stop the night sweat from happening [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
- **Supplements and herbal remedies**: :grade{id="otc-supplements-vms"}. The Menopause Society does not recommend them for hot flashes or night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Black cohosh, DHEA and soy isoflavones are not proven to work, and some carry risks such as liver damage [@nia-hot-flashes-what-can-i-do].

## When to see a doctor and what to ask

See a doctor if night sweats regularly wake you up or worry you [@nhs-night-sweats]. Make an appointment soon if you also have any of these:

- a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery [@nhs-night-sweats]
- a cough or diarrhea [@nhs-night-sweats]
- weight loss you can't explain [@nhs-night-sweats; @medlineplus-sweating]
- a fast, pounding heartbeat with the sweating [@medlineplus-sweating]
- heavy sweating that lasts a long time or has no clear reason [@medlineplus-sweating]

:::urgent
**Call 911** if sweating comes with chest pain, pressure or tightness, pain that spreads to your jaw, left arm or back, or chest discomfort with shortness of breath, dizziness, nausea or a racing heart [@medlineplus-chest-pain]. Also call 911 if a racing or pounding heartbeat doesn't go away, or comes with shortness of breath or fainting [@nhs-heart-palpitations].

If you're in the UK: call 999 or go to A&E for these symptoms. If they have stopped, call NHS 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment [@nhs-heart-palpitations].
:::
