Waking at the same early hour and struggling to get back to sleep is common in perimenopause, and night-time hot flashes are often behind it, even ones you don't notice [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018]. Tonight, keep your bedroom cool and skip late caffeine and alcohol [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause]. CBT for sleep and hormone therapy have the strongest evidence [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Why it happens

If you fall asleep easily but then wake hours later, that is a form of [insomnia](/symptoms/insomnia/), which means trouble falling or staying asleep [@nia-sleep-older-adults]. In [perimenopause](/perimenopause/), several things can feed into it.

- **Hot flashes you may not notice**: In a sleep-lab study of 34 perimenopausal women, about 7 in 10 hot flashes picked up by skin sensors came with an awakening. Waking linked to hot flashes made up about a quarter of their total time awake at night [@de-zambotti-hot-flashes-sleep-2014]. Women often under-report night hot flashes, because remembering them the next morning is unreliable [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018]. So "no sweat, still awake" doesn't rule out a [night sweat](/symptoms/night-sweats/).
- **Which comes first is not always clear**: Research suggests that waking up may itself trigger a hot flash, rather than the other way around [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
- **Changing hormones**: Long-term studies link sleep problems with menopause stage and changing hormone levels, beyond the effect of age. In one large US study, women with moderate to severe hot flashes were almost three times as likely to report waking often at night [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018].
- **An earlier body clock**: Limited research suggests the body clock may shift earlier after menopause, which could add to early-morning waking [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018].
- **Part of a wider cluster**: A 2026 clinical review notes that poor sleep in perimenopause often comes with tiredness, mood changes and trouble concentrating, as an overlapping group of symptoms linked to shifting hormones [@stute-nonhormonal-transition-2026].

## Other causes a doctor can check

Not every early waking is hormonal. A doctor can check for other causes, such as:

- **Sleep apnea**: This means repeated pauses in breathing during sleep. In one US study, postmenopausal women were about 2.6 times as likely as premenopausal women to have it, after allowing for age, weight and smoking [@young-menopause-sleep-disordered-breathing-2003]. In women it often shows up as insomnia, tiredness or headaches; loud snoring is more common in men [@nhlbi-sleep-apnea-symptoms].
- **Low mood or depression**: Waking too early in the morning is one of the signs of depression, along with low mood, loss of interest and trouble concentrating [@nimh-depression-booklet]. See [menopause depression](/symptoms/depression/).
- **Stress and worry**: Stress, anxiety and going over things in your head are linked with insomnia in midlife women [@baker-sleep-menopausal-transition-review-2018]. See [menopause anxiety](/symptoms/anxiety/).
- **Caffeine and alcohol**: Caffeine late in the day can keep you awake, and even small amounts of alcohol make it harder to stay asleep [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
- **Waking to pee**: Mention this to your doctor. Waking often at night to urinate is one of the listed signs of sleep apnea [@nhlbi-sleep-apnea-symptoms]. See [bladder urgency](/symptoms/bladder-urgency/).

## Try tonight

These steps come from US National Institute on Aging advice for sleep problems around menopause [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].

:::try
1. Keep your bedroom at a comfortable temperature, not too hot or too cold, and as quiet as you can [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
2. Avoid caffeine (coffee, tea, chocolate) late in the day [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
3. Skip the nightcap: even small amounts of alcohol make it harder to stay asleep [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
4. Go to bed and get up at the same time each day, and avoid naps in the late afternoon or evening [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
5. Keep the TV, computer and phone out of the bedroom [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
:::

## What can help (graded by evidence)

| Option | Evidence grade | What guidelines say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**, including menopause-specific CBT | :grade{id="cbt-sleep-vms"} | NICE suggests menopause-specific CBT for night waking linked to hot flashes; The Menopause Society rates CBT Level I for hot flashes [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |
| **[Hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/)** | :grade{id="mht-sleep-vms"} | The most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats, so it helps most when they are what wakes you [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] |
| **[Non-hormonal prescription options](/treatments/nonhormonal/)** (gabapentin, SSRIs/SNRIs) | :grade{id="nonhormonal-rx-sleep-vms"} | Level I for hot flashes and night sweats [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |
| **Breathable sleepwear and bedding** | :grade{id="cooling-comfort"} | Can make you more comfortable, but does not stop the hot flashes themselves [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |
| **Over-the-counter [magnesium](/does-it-work/magnesium/) and herbal sleep supplements** | :grade{id="otc-sleep-supplements-vms"} | Supplements and herbal remedies are not recommended by The Menopause Society (2023) for hot flashes (Levels I–II) [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy] |

Sleeping pills, including over-the-counter aids such as melatonin, may help for a short time. They are not a cure for insomnia and should not be used long term [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].

## Track your wake times

Keep a sleep diary for a couple of weeks. Note when you go to bed, when you wake in the night and when you get up, plus any caffeine, alcohol or exercise that day [@nia-sleep-older-adults]. Add whether you felt hot when you woke. Our [private symptom tracker and doctor report](/tools/symptom-tracker/) can hold this, so your clinician has something concrete to work from.

## When to see a doctor

- **Sleep changes haven't helped**: Talk to your doctor if you are still having trouble sleeping. CBT for insomnia (a type of talking therapy) has been shown to help women with menopausal symptoms [@nia-sleep-problems-menopause].
- **Signs of sleep apnea**: Breathing that stops and starts, gasping for air, loud snoring or daytime sleepiness are worth raising; you may need a sleep study [@nhlbi-sleep-apnea-symptoms]. Researchers advise checking menopausal women for sleep apnea when they have snoring, daytime sleepiness or unrefreshing sleep [@young-menopause-sleep-disordered-breathing-2003]. Untreated sleep apnea can lead to serious health problems, including heart attack [@nia-sleep-older-adults].
- **Low mood that won't lift**: Early waking together with low mood or loss of interest most of the day, nearly every day, for 2 weeks or more needs a check for depression [@nimh-depression-booklet].
