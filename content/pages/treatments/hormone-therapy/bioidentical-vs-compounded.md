"Bioidentical" means a hormone has the same structure as the one your body makes, and several FDA-approved HRT products already are [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. "Compounded" means custom-mixed by a pharmacy without FDA approval or standard testing [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nasem-compounded-bht-2020]. Compounded products haven't been shown to be safer, so FDA-approved hormones are recommended first [@acog-compounded-bioidentical-2023].

## What's the difference between bioidentical and compounded hormones?

They describe two different things, and marketing often blurs them. "Bioidentical" is about the hormone's chemical structure. "Compounded" is about how and where the product is made [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

| | FDA-approved bioidentical HRT | Compounded "bioidentical" hormones |
|---|---|---|
| What's in it | Estradiol, estrone, or micronized progesterone [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] | May mix several hormones, such as estradiol, estrone, estriol, DHEA, testosterone, and progesterone, in untested combinations [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] |
| Regulation | Regulated and monitored for purity and how well it works [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] | Not reviewed or approved by the FDA for safety, quality, or effectiveness [@nasem-compounded-bht-2020] |
| Dose consistency | Standardized [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] | Can be too strong, too weak, or contaminated because there's no standard [@nasem-compounded-bht-2020] |
| Safety information | Comes with a package insert based on clinical trials [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] | No label outlining risks [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] |
| Forms | Standard forms such as patches, gels, pills, and vaginal products [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] | May include untested forms such as pellets under the skin or troches (lozenges) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy] |

In short, being bioidentical doesn't require being compounded. You can get bioidentical estradiol and progesterone as FDA-approved prescriptions. See [types of HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/types/) for the approved options.

## Are bioidentical hormones safer?

Not in the way marketing suggests. ACOG says compounding pharmacies often use the term to suggest their products are natural, and so safer and more effective than FDA-approved HRT, but evidence to support those claims is lacking [@acog-compounded-bioidentical-2023]. On our scale, compounded "bioidentical" hormones are rated :grade{id="compounded-bioidentical"}.

Compounded products bring their own risks. The Menopause Society lists minimal regulation, doses that are too high or too low, impurities, lack of sterility, a lack of safety and effectiveness data, and no label of risks [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE says the effectiveness and safety of unregulated hormone preparations are unknown [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## What do medical groups recommend?

Major groups agree that FDA-approved hormones should come first:

- **ACOG (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists)**: Compounded bioidentical HRT should not be prescribed routinely when FDA-approved options exist. If you still want it, your clinician should explain the lack of FDA approval and the risks specific to compounding [@acog-compounded-bioidentical-2023].
- **The Menopause Society**: Your preference alone shouldn't be the reason to use compounded hormones, especially when approved bioidentical products are available [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **The National Academies (2020)**: In a report requested by the FDA, an expert committee found too little evidence that compounded bioidentical hormones are clinically useful [@nasem-compounded-bht-2020].

More research is needed: ACOG notes that high-quality trials comparing compounded products with FDA-approved HRT are still needed [@acog-compounded-bioidentical-2023].

## When might a compounded hormone make sense?

Rarely, and for specific medical reasons. The National Academies recommend limiting compounded hormones to two situations [@nasem-compounded-bht-2020]:

- **An allergy**: You're allergic to an ingredient in an FDA-approved product.
- **A form that isn't made**: You need a dose or form that no FDA-approved product offers.

The Menopause Society gives the same two reasons, and says prescribers should document why a compounded product is needed [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Do I need a saliva or blood test to get bioidentical hormones?

No. Some compounded hormone plans set doses from saliva, urine, or blood tests, but The Menopause Society says saliva and urine testing for dosing is unreliable and not recommended, and blood hormone testing is rarely needed [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Hormone levels and absorption vary through the day and between people, so these tests can't reliably guide dosing [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Are hormone pellets bioidentical?

They can be, but that doesn't make them tested or approved. The Menopause Society lists pellets under the skin as one of the nonstandard, untested ways compounded bioidentical hormones are given [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. The National Academies recommended that compounded pellets be considered for the FDA's list of drugs that are difficult to compound safely, because of safety concerns with how they release hormones [@nasem-compounded-bht-2020]. ## What this means for you

If you're considering a compounded "bioidentical" product, including pellets, custom creams, or troches, ask whether an FDA-approved option could treat the same symptoms. Many of the same hormones are available with standard testing and labeling [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
