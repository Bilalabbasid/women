HRT pills (estrogen taken by mouth) raise the risk of blood clots in the veins, while HRT patches, gels and sprays do not appear to [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nhs-hrt-benefits-risks-2023]. If you are at higher risk of clots, NICE advises considering HRT through the skin rather than tablets [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Does HRT cause blood clots?

HRT taken as a pill can cause blood clots, but HRT through the skin has not been shown to: NICE says clot risk is raised with oral HRT and not raised with transdermal HRT (patch, gel or spray) [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The clots in question are venous thromboembolism (VTE): a clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg (DVT), which can travel to the lungs (pulmonary embolism) [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

Even with pills, the added risk is small. The Menopause Society calls the extra clot risk with HRT rare, and the NHS says the risk with HRT tablets is still very low [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nhs-hrt-benefits-risks-2023]. Raised estrogen levels are one of the known risk factors for clots, alongside older age, family history and long periods without moving [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

## Patch vs. pill: which has the lower clot risk?

Patches, gels and sprays have the lower clot risk: NICE says VTE risk is greater with oral than with transdermal HRT, and not raised with transdermal HRT at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

| Type of HRT | Blood clot (VTE) risk | Stroke risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pill (oral estrogen)** | Raised [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. In a large UK study, about 1.6 times the odds of a clot [@vinogradova-hrt-vte-2019]. | Raised, more so with higher doses, longer use and older age at starting [@nice-ng23-recommendations] |
| **Patch, gel or spray (transdermal)** | Not raised [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nhs-hrt-benefits-risks-2023]. No link found in the UK study [@vinogradova-hrt-vte-2019]. | Unlikely to rise [@nice-ng23-recommendations] |
| **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** | Does not carry the usual risks of HRT [@nhs-types-of-hrt] | Does not carry the usual risks of HRT [@nhs-types-of-hrt] |

The route comparison comes from observational studies; trials comparing patches and pills head to head for clots have not been done [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. For the day-to-day differences between routes, see our [patch vs. gel vs. pill comparison](/treatments/hormone-therapy/patch-vs-pill/).

## What are the signs of a blood clot on HRT?

The warning signs are the same as for any clot: pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one leg or arm, or sudden breathlessness or chest pain if a clot reaches the lungs [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025]. A stroke, which oral HRT can also make slightly more likely, shows up as sudden weakness, speech or vision problems [@cdc-stroke-signs-2026; @nice-ng23-recommendations].

:::urgent
**Call 911** if you have sudden, sharp chest pain with shortness of breath, especially if one leg is swollen [@medlineplus-chest-pain]. Seek help right away for other signs of a clot in the lungs: trouble breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, coughing up blood, or feeling lightheaded or faint [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

**Call 911** for sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm or leg (especially on one side), confusion or trouble speaking, trouble seeing, loss of balance, or a sudden severe headache with no known cause: these can be signs of a stroke [@cdc-stroke-signs-2026].

**See a doctor as soon as possible** for swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth, or redness in one leg or arm, which can be signs of a deep vein clot [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

If you're in the UK: call 999 or go to A&E for leg pain or swelling with shortness of breath or chest pain. For a possible leg clot on its own, ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 [@nhs-dvt-2026].
:::

## Who is more likely to get a clot on HRT?

Your starting risk matters more than HRT alone. NICE singles out people already at higher clot risk, including those with a body mass index (BMI) over 30, and advises considering transdermal HRT for them [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

- **Family history or inherited clotting conditions**: NICE advises referral to a blood specialist (hematologist) before considering HRT for people at high risk, such as those with a strong family history of clots or a hereditary thrombophilia (an inherited tendency to clot) [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Age and timing**: Starting HRT more than 10 years after menopause, or after 60, carries greater absolute risks of clots and stroke [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Other risk factors**: Older age, family history, a vein injury and slow blood flow from not moving raise clot risk for anyone [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

More in [who HRT may suit, and who needs specialist advice](/treatments/hormone-therapy/is-it-right-for-me/).

## Can you take HRT if you've had a blood clot?

It needs a specialist decision, and some products rule it out. The label for one oral progesterone capsule, for example, lists an active or past DVT or pulmonary embolism as a reason not to use it [@prometrium-prescribing-information]. NICE advises that people at high clot risk see a hematologist before HRT is considered at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

For people at increased risk who do go ahead, the NHS says patches, spray or gel are usually advised instead of tablets [@nhs-hrt-benefits-risks-2023]. Non-hormonal treatments are another option; see our [non-hormonal treatments overview](/treatments/nonhormonal/).

## Does HRT raise stroke risk?

Only with pills, and only slightly: NICE says stroke risk rises with oral estrogen but is unlikely to rise with transdermal estrogen, and the baseline risk for women under 60 is very low [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. NICE adds that the increase with oral combined HRT may be greater in Black people [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

In numbers, NICE estimates about 3 strokes per 1,000 women aged 50 to 54 over 5 years without HRT [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024]. With oral estrogen-only HRT that becomes about 4 per 1,000; with HRT through the skin, the numbers are similar to no HRT [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024]. With oral combined HRT the rise is real but too small to show in groups of 1,000 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024]. See [heart health after menopause](/postmenopause/heart-health/) for the wider picture.

## Does the type of estrogen or progestogen matter?

It may, although the evidence is mostly observational. In the large UK study, estradiol pills carried a lower clot risk than conjugated equine estrogen pills, and conjugated equine estrogen with medroxyprogesterone acetate carried the highest [@vinogradova-hrt-vte-2019].

- **Dose**: Lower doses of oral estrogen may carry less clot risk than higher doses, though trials comparing them are lacking [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE recommends the lowest dose that works [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Progestogen**: The Menopause Society says micronized progesterone is potentially less likely to promote clots than other progestogens, but trials have not been designed to test this [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in [progesterone in HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/progesterone/).
