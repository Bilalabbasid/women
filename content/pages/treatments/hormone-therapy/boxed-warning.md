In 2026 the FDA began removing warnings about heart disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia from the boxed warning (the "black box") on hormone therapy labels [@fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026]. Whole-body estrogen-only products keep a boxed warning about uterine cancer, and labels for whole-body products still describe heart and breast cancer risks [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]. Hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

The FDA approved the first six updated labels on February 12, 2026 [@fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026]. Whole-body (systemic) estrogen-alone products still carry the boxed warning about endometrial (uterine) cancer [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025; @hhs-mht-fact-sheet-2025]. For the other product types, the boxed warning was removed entirely [@public-citizen-mht-labels-2026]. For the bigger picture, see our guide to [menopause treatment options](/treatments/).

## Timeline of the change

- **November 10, 2025**: The FDA asked makers of all hormone therapy products, both systemic and vaginal, to change their labels [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **By February 2026**: 29 drug companies had submitted proposed label changes [@fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026].
- **February 12, 2026**: The FDA approved the first six updated labels. They cover four product types: systemic combination (estrogen plus progestogen), systemic estrogen-alone, systemic progestogen-alone, and vaginal estrogen [@fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026].

## What was removed from the boxed warning?

For all hormone therapy products, systemic and vaginal, the FDA asked makers to make these changes [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]:

- **Heart disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia**: This language comes out of the boxed warning [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025; @fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026]. The FDA also asked for the probable dementia warning to come off the labels [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Endometrial cancer**: This boxed language comes off every product type except systemic estrogen-alone products [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **"Lowest effective dose for the shortest amount of time"**: This long-standing recommendation is removed [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].

## What was added?

- **Timing for systemic products**: Labels add consideration of starting hormone therapy for moderate to severe hot flashes in women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025; @hhs-mht-fact-sheet-2025]. Our page on [HRT timing](/treatments/hormone-therapy/timing/) explains why timing matters.
- **Data for women in their 50s**: Systemic labels add Women's Health Initiative (WHI) results for women aged 50 to 59 [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Shorter labels for vaginal products**: Safety information for [vaginal estrogen](/treatments/hormone-therapy/vaginal-estrogen/) is condensed to focus on what matters most for a product used locally [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].

## What remains in the warnings?

The FDA did not say hormone therapy is risk-free. These warnings stay:

- **Endometrial cancer (systemic estrogen-alone)**: The boxed warning stays on these products [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025; @hhs-mht-fact-sheet-2025]. The reason: in someone with a uterus, whole-body estrogen taken without a progestogen (a progesterone-like hormone) raises the risk of endometrial cancer, so The Menopause Society recommends adding a progestogen [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Heart disease and breast cancer (systemic products)**: This information is kept in the labels, just no longer in the boxed warning [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Who usually shouldn't use hormone therapy**: The Menopause Society lists conditions that rule out both oral and transdermal hormone therapy. These include unexplained vaginal bleeding, liver disease, a past estrogen-sensitive cancer such as breast cancer, past heart disease, stroke, heart attack or blood clot, or a personal history or inherited high risk of clots [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Why did the FDA change it?

In 2003, the FDA began approving label changes for all of these products based on findings from the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) trials, and these changes eventually added the boxed warning [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]. Both WHI trials began in the 1990s and were stopped early, the estrogen-plus-progestin trial in 2002 and the estrogen-alone trial in 2004 [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].

The FDA now says it has reconsidered the benefit and risk of these drugs [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]. Its reasons:

- **Older trial participants**: The WHI trials mainly looked at heart disease and other long-term conditions in postmenopausal women of all ages, and the average participant was 63 [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]. That is older than most women who start hormone therapy for symptoms [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Women going without treatment**: The FDA says it is concerned that many women with symptoms who could benefit are not being prescribed hormone therapy [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Expert input**: The FDA held an expert panel on July 17, 2025, and then asked for public comment [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].

This fits The Menopause Society's 2022 position. For women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause who have no reason to avoid it, the benefits of hormone therapy generally outweigh the risks. Starting later is less favorable because the risks of heart disease, stroke, blood clots, and dementia are higher [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Not everyone agrees with the change

Expert groups broadly support the change for vaginal estrogen but disagree about systemic hormone therapy:

- **The Menopause Society**: Agrees with removing the boxed warning from low-dose vaginal estrogen. It says systemic estrogen still has potential risks for some people that should be reviewed in detail. Those risks are low for younger, healthy women who start closer to menopause, and greater for women who start older or further from menopause [@menopause-society-fda-comment-2025].
- **The International Menopause Society**: Supports removing the boxed warning from vaginal estrogen, but does not support removing all warnings from systemic hormone therapy. It notes that systemic therapy can raise heart disease risk, breast cancer, and dementia in some groups, especially women who start later or already have these conditions [@ims-statement-boxed-warning-2025].
- **Public Citizen (a consumer advocacy group)**: Argues the change was based on opinion rather than new safety data, and was made without a formal, independent advisory committee [@public-citizen-mht-labels-2026; @public-citizen-mht-warnings-2025]. It also says officials promoted unapproved uses, such as preventing heart disease. And it points out that the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force advises against hormone therapy to prevent chronic conditions [@public-citizen-mht-warnings-2025].

## What this means for your appointment

The label change doesn't decide whether hormone therapy is right for you. That still depends on your age, how long it's been since menopause, your symptoms, and your health history [@menopause-society-fda-comment-2025; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. If a clinician once ruled out [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) because of the boxed warning, the new labels are a fair reason to ask again. You can track hot flashes in our [symptom diary and report](/tools/symptom-tracker/), build an appointment sheet with our [treatment conversation builder](/tools/treatment-questions/), and compare non-prescription options in our [Does It Work? directory](/does-it-work/).

## When to see a doctor

See your doctor about any vaginal bleeding after menopause, even a small amount or a single episode. It's usually not serious, but it should be checked [@nhs-postmenopausal-bleeding]. If you take hormone therapy, get help urgently for signs of a stroke or blood clot.

:::urgent
**Call 911 right away** for signs of a stroke [@cdc-stroke-signs]:

- Sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body
- Sudden confusion, trouble speaking, or trouble understanding speech
- Sudden trouble seeing, or sudden trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance
- A sudden severe headache with no known cause

**Seek medical help immediately** for possible signs of a blood clot in the lungs: trouble breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that gets worse when you breathe in deeply or cough, coughing up blood, or feeling lightheaded or fainting [@cdc-vte-about].

**See a doctor as soon as possible** for swelling, pain, warmth, or redness in a leg or arm, which can be signs of a clot [@cdc-vte-about].

If you're in the UK: call 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111 for urgent advice.
:::
