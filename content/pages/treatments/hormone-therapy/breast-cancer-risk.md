Combined HRT (estrogen plus a progestogen) slightly raises breast cancer risk, and the rise grows the longer you take it; estrogen-only HRT raises it very little or not at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. In NICE's estimates, 5 years of combined HRT from age 50 means about 20 extra breast cancers per 1,000 women by age 69 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024].

## Does HRT cause breast cancer?

HRT is linked to a small rise in breast cancer risk, and how big depends mostly on the type: combined HRT raises it, while estrogen-only HRT raises it very little or not at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. A 2019 analysis of the worldwide studies found extra risk with every type of hormone therapy except vaginal estrogen, and the extra risk was larger for combined than for estrogen-only products [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019].

Most of this evidence comes from observational studies, which show a link but cannot fully prove cause [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019]. The Menopause Society describes the overall risk as rare: less than 1 extra case per 1,000 women for each year of use [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## How much does HRT raise breast cancer risk?

NICE gives the risk as the number of women out of 1,000 who would develop breast cancer between ages 50 and 69, with and without HRT started at 50 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024]:

| HRT use from age 50 | Breast cancer by age 69, per 1,000 women | Compared with no HRT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **No HRT** | 59 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] | Baseline [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] |
| **Combined HRT for 5 years** | 79 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] | 20 more [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] |
| **Combined HRT for 10 years** | 92 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] | 33 more [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] |
| **Estrogen-only HRT for 5 years** | 69 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] | 10 more [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] |
| **Estrogen-only HRT for 10 years** | 71 [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] | 12 more [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024] |

These are population averages, not a personal forecast, and the combined figures pool sequential and continuous regimens [@nice-ng23-hrt-discussion-aid-2024]. The 2019 analysis came to similar sizes: 5 years from age 50 would add about 1 case in every 50 users of estrogen plus a daily progestogen, 1 in 70 with a progestogen taken part of the month, and 1 in 200 with estrogen only [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019]. Ten years of use roughly doubled those extra cases [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019].

## Estrogen-only vs. combined HRT: which has the lower breast cancer risk?

Estrogen-only HRT carries the lower breast cancer risk: NICE says there is very little or no increase with it, compared with a clear, duration-related increase with combined HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. But estrogen-only HRT is only offered after a total hysterectomy, because with a uterus it raises the risk of womb lining (endometrial) cancer [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

The largest randomized trial points the same way. In the Women's Health Initiative (WHI), women after a hysterectomy who took estrogen alone had fewer breast cancers than those on placebo, and fewer breast cancer deaths, over more than 20 years of follow-up [@chlebowski-whi-breast-cancer-2020]. Women with a uterus who took estrogen plus a progestin had more breast cancers than those on placebo [@chlebowski-whi-breast-cancer-2020]. Observational studies, by contrast, show a small increase with estrogen-only HRT [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

- **Type of estrogen**: NICE says breast cancer risk is similar with estradiol and with conjugated equine estrogen [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **More on the choice**: see [types of HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/types/) for who is offered which type.

### Does the type of progestogen matter?

The schedule matters, while the effect of the specific progestogen is still unclear. NICE says sequential combined HRT (progestogen for part of each cycle) carries a lower breast cancer risk than continuous combined HRT (progestogen every day), though both carry more than no HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The 2019 analysis also found higher risk with a daily progestogen than with one taken less often [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019].

Whether micronized progesterone or dydrogesterone carries less breast cancer risk than other progestogens is not known: NICE says the evidence is insufficient, and The Menopause Society notes trials have not been designed to answer it [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in [progesterone in HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/progesterone/).

## Does the risk go away after you stop HRT?

The extra risk falls after you stop, but not straight away: NICE says it declines after stopping combined HRT and persists for at least 10 years [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The 2019 analysis found that how much risk lingers depends on how long HRT was used, with little extra risk after less than 1 year of use [@collaborative-group-mht-breast-cancer-2019].

This is one reason the NHS suggests not taking HRT for longer than you need it to control symptoms [@nhs-hrt-benefits-risks-2023]. See [starting and stopping HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/starting-and-stopping/) for how that decision is reviewed.

## Your own starting risk matters

Breast cancer risk varies from person to person, with or without HRT, depending on factors you can and cannot change [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The Menopause Society says the relative effect of HRT looks similar in women at average and at high risk, but the actual number of extra cases is greater when the starting risk is higher [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

That is why NICE advises that people at high risk of breast cancer get information on all their options and a referral to a menopause specialist [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Can you take HRT after breast cancer?

Usually not: the American Cancer Society says doctors generally do not recommend hormone therapy after breast cancer, though there can be exceptions [@acs-mht-after-breast-cancer-2025]. The risk is clearly raised after hormone receptor-positive cancer; after receptor-negative cancer it is less clear, and some doctors may consider it if non-hormonal treatments have not helped [@acs-mht-after-breast-cancer-2025].

NICE advises stopping systemic HRT if breast cancer is diagnosed [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Options for symptoms are covered in our guide for [breast cancer survivors](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/).

## Did the FDA label change remove the breast cancer risk?

No. The FDA asked companies to take breast cancer language out of the boxed warning on hormone therapy labels, but did not ask them to remove breast cancer risk from the label's Warnings and Precautions section [@fda-hrt-consumer-update-2026]. See [the FDA boxed warning change, explained](/treatments/hormone-therapy/boxed-warning/) for what changed and why medical groups disagree.
