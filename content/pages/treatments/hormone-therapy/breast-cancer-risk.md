Breast cancer risk is usually the first question women ask about [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/), and the honest answer depends on which type of HRT you're asking about. NICE guideline NG23 draws a clear distinction between **combined HRT** (estrogen plus a progestogen) and **estrogen-only HRT** (used by people who have had a total hysterectomy) [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Combined HRT (estrogen plus progestogen)

- **Risk increases with combined HRT**, and that increase rises with how long you use it, is higher while currently taking it than after stopping, and persists for **at least 10 years after stopping** even though it declines over that time [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- There is also a **very small increase in risk of death from breast cancer** with combined HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **The regimen matters:** sequential combined HRT (progestogen for part of the cycle) carries a lower risk than continuous combined HRT (progestogen every day) — though both carry more risk than no HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- There currently isn't enough evidence to say whether **micronised progesterone or dydrogesterone** change this risk compared with other progestogens [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Estrogen-only HRT (after a total hysterectomy)

- NICE guidance states there is **very little or no increase** in breast cancer risk, and **little or no increase** in breast cancer mortality, with estrogen-only HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- Risk is **similar** between oestradiol and conjugated equine oestrogen formulations [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## It's not just about HRT

Breast cancer risk varies from person to person based on factors that have nothing to do with hormone therapy — family history, genetics, age, alcohol use, body weight, and others all play a role [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. That's why this is a conversation to have with a clinician who knows your personal and family history, not a single number that applies to everyone equally.

NICE's own patient materials include a **discussion aid** with the actual number of additional breast cancer cases per 1,000 people over 5- and 10-year periods, broken down by HRT type — ask your clinician to go through this with you rather than relying on a headline percentage.

## Where this fits with the 2025–2026 label changes

The FDA's 2025–2026 boxed-warning changes removed breast cancer language from the boxed warning on several products — see [the FDA boxed warning change, explained](/treatments/hormone-therapy/boxed-warning/) for what changed, what stayed, and where medical societies disagree about whether that was the right call.
