Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is prescription estrogen, with a progestogen added if you still have a uterus. It is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats, and it helps prevent bone loss [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nice-ng23-recommendations]. For healthy people who start before 60 or within 10 years of menopause, benefits generally outweigh risks [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Doctors also call it menopausal hormone therapy (MHT). On our scale, its evidence for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/) is graded :grade{id="mht-vms"}. This page covers what HRT is, who it helps, its benefits and risks under the current FDA labels, the main types, who shouldn't take it, and how to start.

## What is HRT?

HRT is medicine that tops up estrogen to ease menopause symptoms, given with a progestogen (progesterone or a similar hormone) if you have a uterus, because estrogen alone can cause overgrowth of the uterine lining and raise the risk of cancer there [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nice-ng23-recommendations]. It is used by people with a uterus and by people who have had a hysterectomy, in different combinations.

- **Estrogen**: The main ingredient. Options include estradiol, which is identical to the estrogen the ovaries make, and conjugated estrogens [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in [estrogen in HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/estrogen/).
- **Progestogen**: Protects the lining of the uterus. Options include micronized progesterone, which has the same structure as the body's own progesterone, and synthetic progestins [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in [progesterone and progestogens in HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/progesterone/).
- **"Bioidentical" products**: FDA-approved bioidentical hormones, such as estradiol and micronized progesterone, are regulated and come with a full label. Compounded "bioidentical" mixes are not FDA approved and lack safety data [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. See [bioidentical vs. compounded hormones](/treatments/hormone-therapy/bioidentical-vs-compounded/).

## Who does HRT help?

HRT mainly helps people with bothersome hot flashes or night sweats, and the FDA approves it as a first-line treatment for moderate to severe hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. The Menopause Society lists four FDA-approved uses [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Hot flashes and night sweats**: Moderate to severe symptoms. No other medicine or alternative therapy has been found to give more relief [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Preventing bone loss**: HRT prevents bone loss and, in a large trial, reduced fractures. It is approved to prevent osteoporosis, not to treat it; other medicines are preferred once osteoporosis is diagnosed [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. See [bone health after menopause](/postmenopause/bone-health/).
- **Early or surgical menopause**: People whose ovaries stop working early or are removed, including [premature ovarian insufficiency](/groups/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/) and [surgical menopause](/groups/surgical-menopause/) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Vaginal dryness and painful sex**: When these are the only symptoms, low-dose [vaginal estrogen](/treatments/hormone-therapy/vaginal-estrogen/) is preferred over whole-body HRT [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

HRT is not recommended at any age to prevent memory loss or dementia [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

### Can you take HRT during perimenopause?

Yes. NICE advises offering HRT for hot flashes and night sweats linked to menopause, and the NHS describes a type of combined HRT (sequential) for people who are still having regular periods [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nhs-types-of-hrt]. For questions about birth control at this stage, see [contraception in perimenopause](/perimenopause/contraception/).

## Is HRT safe? Benefits and risks

For healthy people under 60 or within 10 years of their last period, The Menopause Society says the benefits of HRT outweigh the risks, and the extra risks are rare: fewer than 10 extra cases per 10,000 women each year [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. The risks depend on the type, dose, route, how long you take it, and when you start [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

- **Cancer of the uterine lining, if you have a uterus**: Estrogen-only HRT raises the risk of endometrial cancer in people with a uterus, whether taken as a pill or through the skin [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. That's why whole-body estrogen-alone products keep a boxed warning for it [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025], and why people with a uterus are offered combined HRT, or a combination product that protects the lining without a progestogen (a tissue-selective estrogen complex) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Blood clots**: Estrogen-alone and combined therapy are each linked to a rare increase in clot risk (venous thromboembolism, or VTE) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE states clot risk rises with HRT pills but not with HRT through the skin, such as a patch or gel [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The Menopause Society notes that no trials have compared the routes head to head [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/).
- **Stroke**: Estrogen-alone and combined therapy are each linked to a rare increase in stroke risk [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE states the risk rises with oral estrogen and is unlikely to rise with estrogen through the skin [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Breast cancer**: NICE states breast cancer risk increases with combined HRT and rises the longer it is used, while estrogen-only HRT causes very little or no increase [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. More in [HRT and breast cancer risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/breast-cancer-risk/).
- **Starting later**: Starting more than 10 years after menopause or after age 60 is less favorable, because the risks of heart disease, stroke, blood clots and dementia are greater [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. The FDA has removed the probable dementia warning from labels [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025], but The Menopause Society's 2022 statement still lists dementia among the higher risks for later starters [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. See [the HRT timing window](/treatments/hormone-therapy/timing/).

## What the FDA label says now

The FDA shortened the boxed warning on HRT in 2025 and 2026, but the main risks are still on the label. On November 10, 2025, it asked makers of menopausal hormone therapy to make these changes [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025]:

- **Boxed warning, all products**: Remove the language on heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia, and the advice to use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Endometrial cancer language comes out of the box too, except on whole-body estrogen-alone products [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Whole label, all products**: Remove the probable dementia warning [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Whole-body products (pills, patches, gels, sprays)**: Add that starting therapy for moderate to severe hot flashes can be considered before age 60 or within 10 years of menopause, and keep the heart and breast cancer information in the warnings [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].
- **Low-dose vaginal estrogen**: Condense the safety information to what matters for a local product [@fda-mht-labeling-request-2025].

On February 12, 2026, the FDA approved the first revised labels for six products; 29 companies had submitted changes by then [@fda-mht-labeling-changes-2026]. The Menopause Society supports removing the boxed warning from low-dose vaginal estrogen but says whole-body estrogen still carries risks for some people [@menopause-society-fda-comment-2025]. The International Menopause Society does not support removing all warnings from whole-body therapy [@ims-statement-boxed-warning-2025], and the consumer group Public Citizen opposed the changes [@public-citizen-mht-labels-2026]. Our [boxed warning explainer](/treatments/hormone-therapy/boxed-warning/) covers the debate.

## Types and forms of HRT

The type you're offered depends mostly on whether you have a uterus: combined HRT if you do, estrogen-only HRT if you've had a total hysterectomy [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

- **Combined HRT**: Estrogen plus a progestogen. Sequential combined HRT adds the progestogen for part of each month and is used while periods continue; continuous combined HRT gives both every day and is usually used after menopause [@nhs-types-of-hrt].
- **Estrogen-only HRT**: Taken every day by people without a uterus [@nhs-types-of-hrt].
- **Whole-body (systemic) forms**: Estrogen can be taken as a pill, a skin patch, gel or spray, or one type of vaginal ring [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. These forms work about equally well for hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Local vaginal estrogen**: Low-dose creams, tablets, inserts and rings treat vaginal and urinary symptoms, with minimal absorption into the blood [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Our [types of HRT guide](/treatments/hormone-therapy/types/) explains each one, and [HRT patch vs. gel vs. pill](/treatments/hormone-therapy/patch-vs-pill/) explains why the route can change clot and stroke risk.

## What are the side effects of HRT?

The more common side effects are nausea, bloating, fluid retention, headaches, breast tenderness, breakthrough bleeding, mood swings (usually from the progestogen) and weight gain [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. If you have a uterus, some bleeding is common in the first 6 months of whole-body HRT and within 3 months of a change in dose or product [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. See [HRT side effects](/treatments/hormone-therapy/side-effects/).

## How long does HRT take to work?

Lower doses of HRT may take 6 to 8 weeks to give enough relief from hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE advises a review at 3 months to check how well a treatment works and how you're getting on with it, then once a year [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## How long can you stay on HRT?

There is no set time limit, and The Menopause Society says HRT does not need to be stopped routinely at 60 or 65 [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Longer use should be for a clear reason, such as hot flashes that persist, with regular check-ins on benefits and risks [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

- **When you stop**: Hot flashes come back in about half of women who stop HRT [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Tapering or stopping at once**: There is no agreement on which is better [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE advises offering a choice of either [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

More in [starting and stopping HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/starting-and-stopping/).

## Who shouldn't take HRT?

The Menopause Society lists these as reasons not to use hormone therapy taken as a pill or through the skin (contraindications) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Unexplained vaginal bleeding**: Bleeding needs to be checked first. See [bleeding after menopause](/postmenopause/bleeding/).
- **Liver disease**: This applies to both pills and skin products [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Past estrogen-sensitive cancer**: This includes [breast cancer](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/) [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Heart disease, stroke or clots in the past**: A history of heart disease, stroke, heart attack or blood clot, or a personal or inherited high risk of clots [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

If any of these apply, our guide to [who HRT may suit, and who needs specialist advice](/treatments/hormone-therapy/is-it-right-for-me/) explains the options, and our [non-hormonal treatments](/treatments/nonhormonal/) section covers alternatives.

## How to start HRT

You start HRT by talking with a doctor, nurse practitioner or other prescriber, and the choice of type, dose and route should be tailored to you [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. The Menopause Society says these choices should be made together with you and looked at again over time [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

- **Before the visit**: Note which symptoms bother you most, and any past cancer, blood clot, stroke, heart disease or liver disease, since these change whether HRT is suitable [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **After you start**: Expect a review at about 3 months, then yearly [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Cost and access**: Prices vary by product and plan; see [how much HRT costs](/care/hrt-cost/), [insurance coverage](/care/insurance/), and our [menopause telehealth comparison](/compare/) if you're considering online care.

## When to see a doctor

- **Bleeding on HRT**: If you have a uterus, NICE advises getting medical help promptly for unscheduled bleeding after the first 6 months of whole-body HRT, or more than 3 months after a change in dose or product [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Symptoms not improving**: NICE advises referral to a menopause specialist if treatment doesn't help or side effects continue [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

:::urgent
**Possible stroke: call 911 right away** if you notice sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg (especially on one side), sudden confusion or trouble speaking or understanding, sudden trouble seeing, sudden trouble walking, dizziness or loss of balance, or a sudden severe headache with no known cause [@cdc-stroke-signs-2026].

**Possible clot in the lungs: get medical help immediately (call 911)** if you have trouble breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that gets worse with a deep breath or cough, coughing up blood, or feel lightheaded or faint [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

**Possible clot in a leg or arm: see a doctor as soon as possible** for swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth, or redness or discoloration in the leg or arm [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

If you're in the UK: call 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111 for urgent advice.
:::
