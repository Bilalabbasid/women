[Hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) comes in more than one form, and the route you take it — not just the decision to take it — changes its safety profile. NICE guideline NG23 recommends this specifically as part of the conversation when you're deciding whether to take HRT at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## The three main routes

- **Pill (oral):** Taken by mouth daily. Convenient, but carries higher blood clot and stroke risk than the skin-based options below [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Patch (transdermal):** Applied to the skin and changed every few days, releasing estrogen steadily. Not associated with increased VTE risk, and unlikely to increase stroke risk [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Gel (transdermal):** Applied to the skin daily. Shares the same favorable clot and stroke profile as the patch, since both deliver estrogen through the skin rather than through the digestive system [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Why the route changes the risk

Oral estrogen passes through the liver before reaching the rest of the body, which affects clotting factors in a way that skin-based (transdermal) estrogen does not. That's the mechanism behind the risk difference NICE guidance describes: **VTE risk is not increased with transdermal HRT**, but **is increased with oral HRT**, and is **greater with oral than transdermal** [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. The same pattern holds for stroke risk, which **is unlikely to increase with transdermal estrogen** but **increases with oral estrogen**, rising further with higher doses and longer use [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. See our dedicated page on [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/) for the full breakdown.

## So which one is right for you?

There's no single "best" route for everyone. Oral pills are simple and familiar, and work well for people without additional clot or stroke risk factors. Transdermal options (patch or gel) are often preferred for people with a higher BMI, a personal or family history of blood clots, or other cardiovascular risk factors, precisely because of the safety profile above. Beyond safety, personal factors matter too — skin sensitivity to patches, consistency with daily gel application, and how well a given route controls your specific symptoms.
