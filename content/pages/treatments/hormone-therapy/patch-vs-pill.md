For blood clot and stroke risk, estrogen through the skin (a patch or gel) looks safer than a pill: UK guidance says patches and gels do not raise clot risk, while pills do [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Both routes ease hot flashes about equally well [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Hormone therapy has the strongest evidence of any hot flash treatment [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

UK guidance from NICE says the route you'd use should be part of the conversation when you're deciding whether to take [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## The three main routes

Estrogen that works throughout the body can be taken by mouth, or through the skin (transdermal) as a patch, gel, or spray [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Pill**: Swallowed, so the estrogen passes through the liver first [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE says pills raise the risk of blood clots and can raise stroke risk [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Patch**: Stuck to the skin, where it releases estrogen. NICE says patches do not raise clot risk and are unlikely to raise stroke risk [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Gel or spray**: Put on the skin. It also goes in through the skin, so NICE's lower-risk findings apply to it too [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

If you have a uterus, the estrogen is usually paired with a progestogen (a progesterone-like hormone) to protect the lining of the uterus. Some progestogens can also be given as a skin patch or through a hormonal IUD [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## Do they work equally well for hot flashes?

Yes, as far as studies can tell. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats :grade{id="mht-vms"} [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. A pooled analysis of studies, cited by The Menopause Society, found no real difference between skin and pill forms of estrogen plus a progestogen for easing hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. So the choice of route is mostly about safety, side effects, and what fits your life.

## Why the route changes the risk

Estrogen taken by mouth goes through the liver before it reaches the rest of the body. Patches, gels, and other skin routes skip that step [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Here is what the guidance says about the risk difference:

- **Blood clots**: NICE says the risk of a blood clot in a vein (venous thromboembolism, or VTE) is not raised with patches or gels, is raised with pills, and is higher with pills than with skin forms [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Stroke**: NICE says stroke risk is unlikely to rise with estrogen through the skin, but does rise with estrogen pills. With pills, the extra risk grows with higher doses and is greater when HRT is started at an older age. For estrogen-plus-progestogen HRT, it also grows with longer use, for example more than 5 years [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. NICE adds that the starting stroke risk for women under 60 is very low [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **How strong is this evidence?**: The Menopause Society says skin forms have not been linked to clots in studies that followed people over time, and that limited data suggest less risk than with pills. It also notes there are no randomized trials comparing the two routes, and no head-to-head data for stroke [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

See our page on [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/) for the full breakdown.

## Other differences between routes

The Menopause Society notes a few other possible differences, all based on more limited evidence [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Sex drive**: If low sex drive is a concern, skin forms may be preferred. Estrogen pills raise a protein in the blood that leaves less testosterone free to work [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Gallstones**: Studies that followed people over time found fewer gallstones with skin forms than with pills, but randomized trials haven't confirmed this [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## When the route matters most

There's no single "best" route for everyone. Guidance does point to some situations where the choice matters more:

- **Higher blood clot risk**: NICE recommends considering a patch or gel rather than pills for people at higher risk of blood clots, including people with a BMI over 30 [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **High clot risk**: For people at high risk, such as a strong family history of clots or an inherited clotting disorder, NICE recommends seeing a blood specialist before considering HRT at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **When neither route is usually used**: The Menopause Society lists conditions that rule out hormone therapy by pill or through the skin. These include unexplained vaginal bleeding, liver disease, a past estrogen-sensitive cancer such as breast cancer, past heart disease, stroke, heart attack or blood clot, or a personal history or inherited high risk of clots [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Beyond safety, practical things matter too: how your skin reacts to a patch, whether a daily routine suits you, and how well a given product controls your symptoms.

## When to see a doctor

Talk with your clinician before switching routes or products, and if your symptoms aren't controlled. Get help urgently if you notice signs of a blood clot or stroke, whichever route you use.

:::urgent
**Call 911 right away** if you or someone else has signs of a stroke [@cdc-stroke-signs]:

- Sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body
- Sudden confusion, trouble speaking, or trouble understanding speech
- Sudden trouble seeing, or sudden trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance
- A sudden severe headache with no known cause

**Seek medical help immediately** for possible signs of a blood clot in the lungs: trouble breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that gets worse when you breathe in deeply or cough, coughing up blood, or feeling lightheaded or fainting [@cdc-vte-about].

**See a doctor as soon as possible** for possible signs of a clot in a leg or arm: swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth, or redness or discoloration [@cdc-vte-about].

If you're in the UK: call 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111 for urgent advice.
:::
