HRT patches release estrogen slowly through the skin, and for blood clot and stroke risk, patches and gels look safer than pills: UK guidance says they do not raise clot risk, while pills do [@emc-evorel-pil-2025; @nice-ng23-recommendations]. Both routes ease hot flashes about equally well [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

UK guidance from NICE says the route you'd use should be part of the conversation when you're deciding whether to take [hormone therapy](/treatments/hormone-therapy/) at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## The three main routes

Estrogen that works throughout the body can be taken by mouth, or through the skin (transdermal) as a patch, gel, or spray [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Pill**: Swallowed, so the estrogen passes through the liver first [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. NICE says pills raise the risk of blood clots and can raise stroke risk [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Patch**: Stuck to the skin, where it releases estrogen. NICE says patches do not raise clot risk and are unlikely to raise stroke risk [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Gel or spray**: Put on the skin. It also goes in through the skin, so NICE's lower-risk findings apply to it too [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

If you have a uterus, the estrogen is usually paired with a progestogen (a progesterone-like hormone) to protect the lining of the uterus. Some progestogens can also be given as a skin patch or through a hormonal IUD [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## HRT patches: how they work

An HRT patch is a sticky patch that releases estradiol, a form of estrogen, slowly through your skin, giving a steady supply between changes [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]. Some patches contain estrogen only; combined patches also contain a progestogen, which people with a uterus need to protect its lining [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026; @nhs-hrt-medicines-2026].

- **Estrogen-only patches**: Used alone after a hysterectomy, or with a separate progestogen (such as capsules or a hormonal IUD) if you have a uterus [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026; @nhs-types-of-hrt].
- **Combined patches**: Contain both hormones. "Sequi" (sequential) brands are for people still having periods, and "Conti" (continuous) brands for after periods have stopped [@nhs-hrt-medicines-2026].
- **Strengths**: Patches come in several strengths. Evorel, for example, comes as 25, 50, 75 and 100 (micrograms of estradiol a day), and its leaflet says 100 micrograms a day is the highest dose [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]. NICE recommends the lowest dose that works [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

### How long do HRT patches take to work?

HRT can take up to 3 months to work fully, and the NHS suggests talking to your doctor if you don't feel better by then [@nhs-utrogestan-2023]. NICE recommends a review at 3 months to check how well treatment is working and how you're tolerating it [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

### Do you still have periods on HRT patches?

It depends on the type. With sequential combined HRT, you take a progestogen for part of each cycle and a bleed is expected afterward; continuous combined HRT, usually started once you've gone a year without a period, doesn't aim for a monthly bleed [@nhs-types-of-hrt]. Irregular bleeding is common in the first 6 months either way, and bleeding beyond that should be checked [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## How to use HRT patches

Follow the leaflet for your brand, since change days and details differ. These are the general rules from the NHS and from one brand's leaflet (Evorel) [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026; @emc-evorel-pil-2025].

### Where to put an HRT patch

Stick it below the waist, such as on your thigh or bottom, and not near your breasts or on cuts, spots or irritated skin [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. The Evorel leaflet adds these tips [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]:

- **Skin**: Choose clean, dry, cool, hairless skin, with no cream, moisturizer or talc on it.
- **Rotate**: Don't use the same spot twice in a row.
- **Clothing**: It can go under loose clothing, but not under elastic or a tight waistband.

The NHS and the leaflet give the below-the-waist rule without explaining it, so ask your pharmacist if you want to use a different area [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026; @emc-evorel-pil-2025].

### How often do you change an HRT patch?

Usually once or twice a week, depending on the brand [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. Evorel is changed twice a week on the same two days, so one patch stays on for 3 days and the next for 4 [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]. Start a new pack as soon as you finish one, without a break [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026].

The Evorel leaflet describes putting a patch on and taking it off like this [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]:

1. Peel off half the backing without touching the sticky side, and press that half onto your skin.
2. Remove the rest of the backing and press the whole patch down with your palm for at least 10 seconds.
3. To remove it, peel it off, fold it in half so it sticks to itself, and keep it away from children and pets. Don't flush it.

### What if an HRT patch falls off?

Put a new patch on and keep to your usual change days [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. If you've just had a bath or shower, wait for your skin to cool first [@emc-evorel-pil-2025].

To help patches stay on, the Evorel leaflet advises applying to clean, dry, cool skin without creams, pressing for at least 10 seconds, avoiding tight waistbands, and not scrubbing the patch in the bath or shower [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]. The leaflets we checked don't mention covering a patch with tape, so ask your pharmacist before trying that.

### What if I forget to change my patch?

Change it as soon as you remember, then carry on with your usual change days [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. You may get some bleeding or spotting afterward [@emc-evorel-pil-2025].

In the US, estradiol patches are sold under several brand and generic names; the FDA says six manufacturers make them [@fda-estradiol-patch-availability-2026].

## HRT patch side effects

The side effect most specific to patches is skin irritation where the patch sits: the NHS lists it as common [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. The Evorel leaflet lists irritated, itchy skin and rash at the patch site as very common (more than 1 in 10 users) and red, puffy skin there as common [@emc-evorel-pil-2025]. Moving the patch to a new spot each time is part of the instructions [@emc-evorel-pil-2025].

Other common estrogen side effects include headaches, breast pain or tenderness, and unexpected bleeding or spotting [@nhs-oestrogen-medicine-2026]. Side effects often ease within a few weeks to 3 months [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. See our full guide to [HRT side effects](/treatments/hormone-therapy/side-effects/).

## What if I can't get my usual HRT patch?

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist: the NHS says they can advise on switching if your usual brand isn't available because of supply shortages [@nhs-utrogestan-2023]. In the US, the FDA said in September 2026 that some people were having trouble getting their usual estradiol patches because demand had risen, and that supply had nearly doubled over the past year [@fda-estradiol-patch-availability-2026].

### How much do HRT patches cost in the UK?

In England, each prescription item usually costs £9.90, and an HRT prescription prepayment certificate costs £19.80 for 12 months of unlimited qualifying HRT items [@nhsbsa-hrt-ppc-medicines; @nhs-prescription-prepayment-certificate-2023]. Evorel, Estradot and FemSeven patches, and several combined patches, are on its list [@nhsbsa-hrt-ppc-medicines]. Prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland [@commons-library-prescription-charges-england-2026]. To see whether it would save you money, try our [HRT prepayment certificate calculator](/tools/hrt-prepayment-certificate-calculator/). For US prices, see our [HRT cost guide](/care/hrt-cost/).

## Do they work equally well for hot flashes?

Yes, as far as studies can tell. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats :grade{id="mht-vms"} [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. A pooled analysis of studies, cited by The Menopause Society, found no real difference between skin and pill forms of estrogen plus a progestogen for easing hot flashes [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. So the choice of route is mostly about safety, side effects, and what fits your life.

## Why the route changes the risk

Estrogen taken by mouth goes through the liver before it reaches the rest of the body. Patches, gels, and other skin routes skip that step [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Here is what the guidance says about the risk difference:

- **Blood clots**: NICE says the risk of a blood clot in a vein (venous thromboembolism, or VTE) is not raised with patches or gels, is raised with pills, and is higher with pills than with skin forms [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Stroke**: NICE says stroke risk is unlikely to rise with estrogen through the skin, but does rise with estrogen pills. With pills, the extra risk grows with higher doses and is greater when HRT is started at an older age. For estrogen-plus-progestogen HRT, it also grows with longer use, for example more than 5 years [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. NICE adds that the starting stroke risk for women under 60 is very low [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **How strong is this evidence?**: The Menopause Society says skin forms have not been linked to clots in studies that followed people over time, and that limited data suggest less risk than with pills. It also notes there are no randomized trials comparing the two routes, and no head-to-head data for stroke [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

See our page on [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/) for the full breakdown.

## Other differences between routes

The Menopause Society notes a few other possible differences, all based on more limited evidence [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]:

- **Sex drive**: If low sex drive is a concern, skin forms may be preferred. Estrogen pills raise a protein in the blood that leaves less testosterone free to work [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].
- **Gallstones**: Studies that followed people over time found fewer gallstones with skin forms than with pills, but randomized trials haven't confirmed this [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

## When the route matters most

There's no single "best" route for everyone. Guidance does point to some situations where the choice matters more:

- **Higher blood clot risk**: NICE recommends considering a patch or gel rather than pills for people at higher risk of blood clots, including people with a BMI over 30 [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **High clot risk**: For people at high risk, such as a strong family history of clots or an inherited clotting disorder, NICE recommends seeing a blood specialist before considering HRT at all [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **When neither route is usually used**: The Menopause Society lists conditions that rule out hormone therapy by pill or through the skin. These include unexplained vaginal bleeding, liver disease, a past estrogen-sensitive cancer such as breast cancer, past heart disease, stroke, heart attack or blood clot, or a personal history or inherited high risk of clots [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Beyond safety, practical things matter too: how your skin reacts to a patch, whether a daily routine suits you, and how well a given product controls your symptoms.

## When to see a doctor

Talk with your clinician before switching routes or products, and if your symptoms aren't controlled. Get help urgently if you notice signs of a blood clot or stroke, whichever route you use.

:::urgent
**Call 911 right away** if you or someone else has signs of a stroke [@cdc-stroke-signs]:

- Sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body
- Sudden confusion, trouble speaking, or trouble understanding speech
- Sudden trouble seeing, or sudden trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance
- A sudden severe headache with no known cause

**Seek medical help immediately** for possible signs of a blood clot in the lungs: trouble breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that gets worse when you breathe in deeply or cough, coughing up blood, or feeling lightheaded or fainting [@cdc-vte-about].

**See a doctor as soon as possible** for possible signs of a clot in a leg or arm: swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth, or redness or discoloration [@cdc-vte-about].

If you're in the UK: call 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111 for urgent advice.
:::
