**Hormone pellets** — small, hormone-containing implants inserted under the skin, typically releasing estrogen or testosterone over several months — are marketed as a convenient alternative to patches, gels, or pills. We don't recommend them, and here's the evidence-based reasoning.

## Why we don't recommend pellets

Hormone pellets are almost always **compounded products**, meaning they're prepared by a compounding pharmacy rather than manufactured as an FDA-approved formulation. ACOG's 2023 clinical consensus specifically addresses compounded hormone products: it recommends **against routinely prescribing** them when FDA-approved formulations exist, and recommends that clinicians counsel patients on the **lack of FDA approval and the risks specific to compounding** if a patient wants one anyway [@acog-compounded-bioidentical-2023]. See our fuller explainer on [bioidentical vs. compounded hormones](/treatments/hormone-therapy/bioidentical-vs-compounded/) for the full reasoning.

NICE guidance reaches a similar point from a different angle, recommending that patients be told plainly that the **efficacy and safety of unregulated hormone preparations are unknown** [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## The specific problem with pellets as a delivery method

Beyond the compounding issue, pellets have a practical drawback that patches, gels, and pills don't: once inserted, a pellet releases its hormone dose over months **without an easy way to adjust or stop it**. If you have an adverse reaction or the dose turns out to be too high, you can't simply remove a patch or skip a pill — you're committed to that release schedule until it's naturally absorbed. Combined with compounding's lack of standardized dosing testing, this makes precise dose control harder than it needs to be.

## What to ask about instead

[FDA-approved patches, gels, and pills](/treatments/hormone-therapy/types/) offer the same hormones with standardized testing and the ability to adjust or stop quickly if needed. If low libido is specifically what you're hoping a testosterone pellet would address, see our dedicated [testosterone](/treatments/hormone-therapy/testosterone/) page for the FDA-unapproved-but-monitored alternative your clinician can offer instead. See also our page on [products the evidence doesn't support](/evidence/products-we-wont-recommend/) for the broader category this falls into, and our [Does It Work? evidence directory](/does-it-work/) for graded non-hormonal alternatives.
