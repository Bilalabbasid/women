The most common HRT side effects are breast tenderness, headaches, nausea, bloating and irregular bleeding or spotting [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy; @nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. They usually fade within a few weeks to 3 months, and bleeding usually settles within 6 months [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. NICE recommends a check-up at 3 months to review how you're getting on [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## What are the most common side effects of HRT?

The side effects people notice most are breast tenderness, headaches, nausea, bloating, fluid retention, mood swings and breakthrough bleeding [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. Some come from the estrogen and some from the progestogen, which is the hormone added to protect the womb lining if you have a uterus [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023; @nice-ng23-recommendations].

How often each one happens varies from person to person and between products. For oral micronized progesterone, the label warns of short-lived dizziness and drowsiness, and says to take it at bedtime [@prometrium-prescribing-information].

## How long do HRT side effects last?

Most HRT side effects are temporary: the NHS says they usually improve over time and often go away after a few weeks [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. It suggests carrying on with treatment for at least 3 months if possible, and speaking to a GP if side effects are severe or last longer than 3 months [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023].

- **Bleeding**: Usually settles within 6 months of starting [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. NICE says bleeding is common in the first 6 months of HRT [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **After a change of dose or product**: Bleeding is also common in the 3 months after any change [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

## Is bleeding on HRT normal?

Some bleeding is normal at first: NICE says vaginal bleeding is a common side effect in the first 6 months of HRT, or within 3 months of changing the dose or product, and you will be asked about it at your 3-month review [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. Bleeding outside those windows should be checked promptly [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

The NHS advises speaking to a GP if irregular bleeding goes on for more than 6 months, gets heavier, or starts after your periods had stopped for a while [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. The Menopause Society also says unscheduled bleeding more than 6 months after starting combined HRT should be investigated [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. If you're already past menopause, see [bleeding after menopause](/postmenopause/bleeding/).

## What can help if side effects don't settle?

HRT can usually be adjusted: the NHS says a GP might suggest a different dose, a different type of HRT, or a different way of taking it, such as switching from tablets to patches [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023].

- **The 3-month review**: NICE recommends reviewing treatment at 3 months for how well it works and how well you tolerate it, then once a year, or sooner if there are side effects [@nice-ng23-recommendations].
- **Bleeding that won't settle**: The NHS says a GP might change your progestogen dose [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023].
- **Ongoing side effects**: NICE advises referral to a menopause specialist if side effects continue or symptoms don't improve [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

For how routes and products differ, see [patch vs. gel vs. pill](/treatments/hormone-therapy/patch-vs-pill/) and [starting and stopping HRT](/treatments/hormone-therapy/starting-and-stopping/).

## Does HRT cause weight gain?

Probably not for most people: the NHS says there's little evidence that most types of HRT make you put on weight, and weight gain around menopause often happens whether or not you take HRT [@nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]. The Menopause Society says combined HRT either has no effect on weight or is linked to less weight gain than in women not taking it [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy].

Bloating and fluid retention are among the more common side effects of HRT, and are worth mentioning at your review [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. More in our guide to [menopause weight gain](/symptoms/weight-gain/).

## What are the serious risks of HRT?

Serious problems are rare. The Menopause Society says both estrogen-only and combined HRT carry a rare increased risk of gallbladder disease, stroke and blood clots, and combined HRT also slightly raises breast cancer risk [@nams-2022-hormone-therapy]. With a uterus, estrogen without a progestogen raises the risk of womb lining (endometrial) cancer [@nice-ng23-recommendations].

How large these risks are depends on the type and route of HRT. See [HRT and blood clot risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/blood-clot-risk/) and [HRT and breast cancer risk](/treatments/hormone-therapy/breast-cancer-risk/).

## When to see a doctor

Contact your doctor promptly, rather than waiting for your next review, for any of these [@nice-ng23-recommendations; @nhs-hrt-side-effects-2023]:

- **Unexpected bleeding**: Bleeding after the first 6 months, or more than 3 months after a dose change, or bleeding that is getting heavier.
- **Severe or lasting side effects**: Side effects that are severe or still there after 3 months.

:::urgent
**Call 911** for sudden, sharp chest pain with shortness of breath, especially if one leg is swollen: this can be a blood clot in the lungs [@medlineplus-chest-pain]. **Call 911** for sudden weakness or numbness on one side, confusion, trouble speaking or seeing, or a sudden severe headache: these can be signs of a stroke [@cdc-stroke-signs-2026].

**See a doctor as soon as possible** for swelling, pain, warmth or redness in one leg or arm, which can be signs of a deep vein clot [@cdc-blood-clots-about-2025].

If you're in the UK: call 999 or go to A&E for leg pain or swelling with shortness of breath or chest pain. For a possible leg clot on its own, ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 [@nhs-dvt-2026].
:::
