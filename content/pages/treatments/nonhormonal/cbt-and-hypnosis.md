Yes. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) reduces how much hot flashes and night sweats bother you, and clinical hypnosis reduces how often hot flashes happen and how severe they are [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. The Menopause Society gives both its top rating (Level I), and neither uses medicine [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy; @owh-menopause-treatment-2026].

## Does CBT help hot flashes?

Yes, CBT helps most with how much hot flashes bother you. In trials, menopause-focused CBT lowered how much women rated their [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes/) and [night sweats](/symptoms/night-sweats/) as a problem, compared with usual care, and the benefit lasted at least 26 weeks [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Its effect on how often hot flashes happen is mixed [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

What the trials found [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]:

- **MENOS 1 and 2**: Group CBT helped 96 breast cancer survivors, and group or self-guided CBT helped 140 women without breast cancer. In both trials, 65% to 78% of women in the CBT groups reached a clearly meaningful improvement, more than with usual care.
- **Other ways of delivering it**: In breast cancer survivors, CBT given by trained nurses (130 women) or over the internet (254 women) reduced both how much hot flashes bothered them and how often they reported them.
- **Not every trial found fewer hot flashes**: In a trial of 422 breast cancer survivors, CBT reduced bother but not frequency.
- **A caveat**: Most CBT trials compared it with usual care or a waiting list, which is a weaker test than comparing it with an equal amount of time and attention.

On our scale, CBT and clinical hypnosis for hot flashes and night sweats are graded :grade{id="cbt-hypnosis-vms"}.

## Does hypnosis help hot flashes?

Yes. In a 2013 trial of 187 postmenopausal women with at least 50 hot flashes a week, 5 weekly sessions of clinical hypnosis cut reported hot flashes by 74%, compared with 17% in a group that got the same time and attention without hypnosis [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Hot flashes measured by a skin monitor fell by 57% versus 10% [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

The results did not depend on whether women expected hypnosis to work [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. In a smaller trial of 60 breast cancer survivors, clinical hypnosis reduced hot flashes and improved mood and sleep more than no treatment [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## CBT or hypnosis: which works better?

No trial in The Menopause Society's 2023 review compared the two directly [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. They were tested for different goals: hypnosis trials showed fewer and milder hot flashes, while CBT trials mainly showed less bother and interference with daily life [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Which suits you may depend on whether the number of hot flashes or how much they disrupt you matters more, and on what is available near you.

A 2024 review of 23 studies (8 of hypnosis and 15 of CBT) points the same way [@muniz-hypnosis-cbt-scoping-review-2024]. Hypnosis consistently reduced how often hot flashes happened, while CBT had mixed effects on how often they happened but helped with the daily disruption and stress they cause [@muniz-hypnosis-cbt-scoping-review-2024]. It was presented at The Menopause Society's 2024 annual meeting as a conference abstract, not a full published paper, so it carries less weight than the trials above [@muniz-hypnosis-cbt-scoping-review-2024].

## What happens in CBT or hypnosis for hot flashes?

Both follow structured programs designed for hot flashes, not general talk therapy or relaxation [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]:

- **CBT**: Learning how hot flashes happen and how thoughts and feelings affect how they feel, relaxation and breathing practice, spotting and changing unhelpful beliefs about hot flashes, and tracking and managing triggers. The self-guided version used a self-help book over 4 weeks, two contacts with a psychologist, weekly homework and a recording for daily relaxation practice.
- **Clinical hypnosis**: A deeply relaxed state with personal mental imagery and suggestion, including suggestions of coolness. The tested program used 5 weekly in-person sessions with a trained clinician, plus self-hypnosis practice at home.

Paced breathing on its own is not recommended for hot flashes, because larger trials found it worked no better than normal breathing [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. See [paced breathing](/does-it-work/paced-breathing/).

## Can you do CBT or hypnosis online or with an app?

Yes, some versions have been tested outside a therapist's office. Internet-delivered CBT, with or without a therapist's support, reduced how much hot flashes bothered breast cancer survivors and how often they reported them [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. In a workplace study of 124 women, CBT from a self-help book reduced how much hot flashes were a problem compared with a waiting list [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. The tested hypnosis program can be given by a trained provider or used through a smartphone app [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].

## Can you use CBT or hypnosis alongside medication or HRT?

Yes. CBT and clinical hypnosis do not use medicine [@owh-menopause-treatment-2026], so there is no drug to interact with other medicines you take. NICE recommends considering menopause-specific CBT for hot flashes, including for people who prefer not to take HRT, and also recommends CBT for sleep problems linked to menopause [@nice-ng23-recommendations]. See our [insomnia](/symptoms/insomnia/) page for more on sleep.

This can matter after [breast cancer](/groups/breast-cancer-survivors/), where hormone therapy may not be suitable and several CBT and hypnosis trials were done in survivors [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. Compare medicine options in our [non-hormonal treatments guide](/treatments/nonhormonal/).

## Finding a program

Look for programs or therapists that describe **menopause-focused CBT** or **clinical hypnosis for hot flashes**, since those structured, symptom-specific programs are what the trials tested [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy]. We have not found reliable published prices; ask your health plan whether it covers these sessions as behavioral health care.

Some other approaches have not held up for hot flashes. The Menopause Society does not recommend mindfulness-based stress reduction, relaxation techniques or [acupuncture](/does-it-work/acupuncture/) as treatments for hot flashes, because the evidence is limited or no better than placebo [@nams-2023-nonhormone-therapy].
